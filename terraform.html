Terraform

Nine thousand square miles, sited on a planet that has two places and no shared clock between them. Every number on this page is computed rather than described, and the first one is the one that matters least flatteringly.

Drawn as a pie chart it is a solid circle with a label pointing at nothing, and that is the chart working rather than failing. Nothing on this page is a plan to terraform Venus. It is a plan to make one small country's worth of it habitable, which is a different claim, is achievable in a way the other is not, and should not borrow the other's name without saying so.

The ladder because the number keeps moving, and it should

Nine thousand is one choice and not an obvious one. Here is what each order of magnitude buys — and the transit time is the figure that changes the character of the thing, since the band moves at about 100 m/s and a platform takes that long to pass any given point on the planet below.

The qualitative break is between the first two. At 9,000 square miles everything on the ecosystem page works, because the settlement can hold one conversation. At 90,000 it needs the assembly: parts of it are hours apart in local conditions and cannot be governed as a single room. At 9,000,000 it is a twelfth of the way around the planet and the two ends have genuinely different weather. Envelope volume scales linearly — ten times the area is ten times the envelope, no worse and no better — so the hard part of scaling is not lift. It is agreement.

The cruise and why slow is correct

The instinct is that a faster transfer is a better one, and for cargo it is exactly backwards. The minimum-energy path is the slow one: a Hohmann transfer to Venus takes around 146 days and costs less propellant than any alternative, and buying that time back costs fuel at a rate which climbs steeply the harder you push.

And there is nothing to buy. Light crosses the same gap in 2.3 to 14.5 minutes depending on the season — about 4.8 at the moment — so anything urgent was never going in the hold. Mass is roughly 45,000 times slower than information on the identical route, and taking a month off five does not change which side of that ratio you are on.

So the cruise should be as leisurely as it can be made. Launch it cheap, launch it often, let transfers stack up in flight; a permanent cycler on a repeating orbit is the limit case of the same idea, where the ship never stops and you pay only to match it. The supply line carries designs at the speed of light and atoms at the speed of patience, and confusing the two is the expensive mistake.

A weak Earth and the fingerprint of what it lost

Venus is 95% of Earth’s diameter, 81.5% of its mass and 90.4% of its surface gravity, made of the same material, at the same age. By bulk properties it is the closest thing to a twin in the solar system and it is not a younger Earth — it is the same age exactly. Not a proto-Earth. A fork.

And it is weak in one specific way that matters here: no intrinsic magnetic field. Earth’s dynamo deflects the solar wind; Venus has only an induced magnetosphere, so the wind has been stripping its upper atmosphere for billions of years.

The fingerprint is in the hydrogen. The ratio of deuterium to ordinary hydrogen on Venus is on the order of a hundred times Earth’s. Deuterium is heavier and escapes more slowly, so an enriched ratio is what is left behind when a great deal of hydrogen has gone to space. That is direct evidence Venus once had far more water than it has, and lost it.

Which changes what this project is. The hydrogen figures on this page — 20 to 30 ppm of water vapour, 2.06% hydrogen in the acid, 136,000 m³ of cloud per kilogram of water — are not the readings of a place that was always dry. They are the readings of a place that had an ocean and does not any more. Bringing water to Venus is not adding something foreign to it. It is putting back the one element the planet could not hold on to, and the reason it could not is that it never had the field to keep it.

92 bar is the pressure at about 910 metres of Earth ocean, and 475 °C is above the melting point of lead. The two sites are fifty kilometres apart and they are not variations on one problem. They are two different engineering disciplines that happen to share a planet.

Site both because the ecosystem already has two layers

What each site holds counted from the panels, not declared

The canopythe aerostat

Everything that works in the air: the fireflies keeping the fast clock, the moths holding a bearing, the butterflies on a route, the bees running carriage, the spiders strung across the gap. All of it is at home in a volume rather than on a surface, which is fortunate, because a volume is what a floating platform is.

The band is warm rather than temperate — 75 °C at 50 km, 27 °C at 55. The comfortable altitude is between the two, and choosing it is choosing between lift and heat: lower is more buoyant and hotter, higher is cooler and thinner.

The floorthe dome

The beetles, the ants, the burial, the litter, the soil. This is the half that gets called the boring one and it is the half that decides whether there is a forest in fifty years, because the canopy is what the floor is currently spending.

It is also the half that has to be built against 92 bar and 475 °C, so the cheap layer biologically is the ruinous one structurally. That inversion is worth sitting with rather than designing around.

The liftboth sites

Fifty kilometres of vertical distance between the canopy and the floor, and material has to move both ways: seed and litter down, soil and minerals up. There is no ecosystem where those two layers are not in contact, and on Venus the contact is a freight problem measured in kilometres of hot column.

The buoyancy arithmetic, since it is the one number people assume is hopeless. At 50 km, breathable air inside masses about 1.067 kg/m³ against 1.594 outside, so net lift is about 0.53 kg per cubic metre. A platform covering the full 23,310 km² at 5 kg/m² of structure needs roughly 2.2 × 10¹¹ m³ of envelope — which is about nine metres of mean depth spread over the footprint. Not a trivial build. Not an absurd one either, and the volume is the habitat rather than a tank bolted to it.

Two clocks, and they differ by twenty-nineboth sites

The surface solar day on Venus is 116.75 Earth days. Sunrise to sunrise, on the ground, four months.

The atmosphere at 50 km laps the planet in about four days. It super-rotates — it goes round vastly faster than the body beneath it — and anything floating freely in it goes round too. A drifting aerostat therefore has a day of roughly four days, and the dome directly below it has one of a hundred and seventeen.

So the canopy and the floor of this forest cannot share a calendar, and the reason is not distance, politics or protocol. It is altitude. It is the same problem as the assembly's table and the relay's fourteen light-minutes, arriving from a third direction, inside one wood — and the answer is the same one: do not negotiate a clock, agree on something outside that neither layer owns.

There is a candidate already on the page. Venus returns to the same place relative to the sun every 583.92 days, and both layers can see the sun. The fast clock stays local, the slow one is shared, and nothing has to be synchronised across fifty kilometres of supercritical CO₂ to make a date stick.

Two things the summaries get wrong and one of them matters

One. You cannot have one atmosphere and room temperature at the same altitude. Nearly every description of the sweet spot says 50–60 km gives "exactly 1 bar" and "0 to 50 °C". Both figures are real and they are five kilometres apart.

1.00 bar is at49.5 kmand it is about 82 °C there.
0–50 °C is at53–58 kmand it is 0.36 to 0.75 bar there.
so you choosepressure or comfortLower is denser, more buoyant, hotter. Higher is cooler, thinner, less lift.

It is not a fatal correction — 0.5 bar is a perfectly workable habitat pressure and cooling 82 °C is easier than cooling 475. It is the sort of thing that gets averaged away in a summary and then designed against as though both numbers were available at once, and this page would rather state the gradient than a headline.

Hydrogen the scarcity that is not manufactured

Everything else there is effectively unlimited. Carbon and oxygen from a 96.5% CO₂ atmosphere, nitrogen at 3.5% of an atmosphere ninety times Earth's, and sunlight at 2,601 W/m², which is 1.91 times what Earth gets. A floating platform sits near the top of the most reflective cloud deck in the solar system and is lit from below as well as above.

And Venus is bone dry. Atmospheric water vapour runs around 20 to 30 parts per million. The hydrogen is in the sulfuric acid droplets, and sulfuric acid is only 2.06% hydrogen by mass against water's 11.19%, so the acid is a poor ore for the one element you cannot do without.

That is what a real scarcity looks like. Nobody arranged it, nobody is holding it back, and no amount of restructuring the market changes the number — it is 2.06% because of what a sulfate is. theory.md argues that scarcity in a core ecosystem is a physical fact and scarcity in a speculative one is manufactured by making fungible things particular. Venus is the clean case of the first: one element, genuinely short, in a place otherwise drowning in raw material.

So the chemistry runs one way. Sabatier and Bosch both take CO₂ and hydrogen and give back water — they are ways of spending hydrogen to move oxygen and carbon around, not ways of getting hydrogen. Every gram of it is either lifted from Earth once or wrung out of acid droplets forever. Design the loop closed or do not design it.

The envelope and the property that makes it safe

outer layerPTFE / fluoropolymerImmune to concentrated sulfuric acid, which is what the cloud is made of.
structural layerVectran / carbon fibreHolds the shape. Vectran has already done this on Mars airbags.

Inside pressure equals outside pressure, and that is the whole safety case. The envelope is not a pressure vessel — it is a bag of one gas floating in another at the same pressure, so there is no explosive stress across the skin and a puncture is a slow exchange rather than a decompression. A tear in something this size leaks over hours or days. You can walk the outside decks in an acid suit and an oxygen mask rather than a pressure suit.

And gravity is 90.4% of Earth's — 8.87 against 9.81 m/s² — which quietly removes the entire category of problem that makes the Moon and Mars hard to live on for decades. It is the least discussed advantage and probably the largest.

Station-keeping is formation flying, not anchoring. Nothing at 50 km holds a position over the ground; the whole band is moving at speed. A 23,310 km² platform is therefore thousands of linked aerostats holding formation with each other while the entire assembly rides the super-rotation — which is also why its day is four days long and the dome underneath it has one of a hundred and seventeen.

Oxygen and who gets to name a thing

The oxygen for all of this comes out of CO₂, which the planet has in quantities that make the word abundant look shy. That part is settled engineering. What is worth a paragraph is the history of the word.

Carl Wilhelm Scheele isolated oxygen around 1771 and did not get it into print for years. Joseph Priestley isolated it independently and published in 1774. Antoine Lavoisier, who was neither first to isolate it nor first to publish, worked out what it actually did — that combustion is combination with this gas rather than the loss of a substance — and named it. The name stuck. Scheele also discovered chlorine, in 1774, and it was named by somebody else too, decades later.

Three different acts. Finding a thing, publishing a thing, and explaining a thing are not the same work and they do not have to be done by the same person — and only one of the three attaches a name. This is not a story about Scheele being robbed: Lavoisier's explanation was the harder and more important piece, and saying otherwise would be its own distortion. It is a story about which act the record keeps.

Which is the argument in theory.md arriving from chemistry rather than from myth: the thing gets named after whoever held the countable half, and the split gets re-explained afterwards as merit. Scheele died at forty-three, probably from handling the substances he was first to isolate. He is the patron of every part of this system that works and is not the part anybody names.

What you cannot bring a footnote about bleach

Sodium hypochlorite is the ordinary answer to keeping a closed habitat clean, and it is the wrong answer here twice over.

It is mostly water. Household bleach is roughly a tenth NaOCl and the rest solution — it does not exist as a dry solid you can ship, and it decomposes on standing even when you keep it wet. On a planet where hydrogen is the one scarce element, a disinfectant that is ninety per cent water is a disinfectant made of the most expensive thing available.

And the outside air is acid. Hypochlorite and acid together release chlorine, which is first-week chemistry and is the reason the two are never stored near each other. The clouds this platform floats in are concentrated sulfuric acid, and the hull is designed on the assumption that some of it gets in. That is an unforced hazard: pick an oxidiser that has no chlorine in it and the whole interaction stops existing.

Sodium itself is not the problem. The element is abundant in basalt and Venus has a great deal of basalt, so it is a surface resource rather than an import. It is the compound, and specifically the water it is dissolved in, that does not survive contact with the actual constraints. Which is the same lesson as everything else on this page: on Venus the question is never whether you can make a thing. It is what the thing costs in hydrogen, and whether it can share a hull with acid.

What this is not the last honest line

This is not terraforming Venus. It is making 0.005% of it habitable in two layers, and calling that terraforming would be exactly the move the rest of this repository objects to — taking a real, checkable, modest number and letting it borrow the name of an enormous one. One part in nineteen thousand seven hundred and forty-four.

The 197 figure is the honest horizon: that is how many of these you would need for one per cent. It is not offered as a discouragement. It is offered because a plan that states its own scale can be believed about everything else, and the forest is real at nine thousand square miles whether or not it is ever nine hundred thousand.
