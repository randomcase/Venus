The format quiet storm · WHUR-FM, Washington, 1976

Melvin Lindsey started it while he was still an intern at Howard, and named it after the title track of Smokey Robinson’s 1975 album A Quiet Storm. By late 1977 it was the most-listened show in the city and every market in the country had one of its own.

Its rule is not the tempo. Its rule is that nothing is announced. No countdown, no top of the hour, no intro over the first bar, no gap between records. The next one is already under the last one before the last one ends. A DJ working that format has exactly one job, which is that the room never drops.

Seven segments on a forty-eight column grid, all of them in row one, and every one starts three columns before the last one ends. They overlap in the grid because they overlap in the set: a crossfade is not a transition between two records, it is a stretch of time during which both are playing. Grid items placed in the same cells stack rather than push each other along, and that stacking is the picture — the drawing is not an illustration of the format, it is the format’s own arithmetic laid out in columns.

gaps is declared, incremented by nothing, and printed — the same instrument as named on the bench. A counter a reader can watch fail to move is worth more than a sentence claiming it would.

And note what the format is: a run with no drop in it has no win condition and no ending. That is what this yard spent fifty pages arriving at in theory.md and what automat.js is built out of — and Lindsey shipped it in 1976, on the radio, in Washington, with two turntables and no theory of fungibility whatsoever.

Caught up Venus · where the phrase is not a metaphor

The solid planet turns once in 243.02 days, backwards. The atmosphere at cloud top does not turn with it: it is carried around at roughly 100 m/s and laps the entire planet in about four and a half days — some sixty times for every one the ground manages. It has a name, superrotation, and after sixty years of measuring it nobody has an agreed account of what keeps it going.

You do not go around Venus. Venus’s air goes around Venus and you are in it. Being caught up is the propulsion budget, and it is the single largest thing the planet gives away free.

To stay above a fixed point you must fly against the whole superrotation, forever, and never stop. The reward is that the ground under you does not change. The board prints 60 above and the arithmetic gives 55, and both are in the room on purpose. Sixty is the figure people quote; fifty-five is what falls out of 100 m/s at 65 km. The wind is not one number — it runs roughly 90–110 m/s across the cloud deck and varies with latitude and altitude — so the honest statement is about four to five days, call it sixty-ish laps, and the way to say that honestly is to show the division and let you see how loose it is. A page that printed one confident number would be claiming a precision the measurement does not have.

The join the same object, described twice

What they share

A medium moving faster than the thing it rests on. It carries whatever is in it. There is no discontinuity anywhere in it, and nothing in it is going anywhere in particular — no arc, no build, no peak, no end. In both cases the passenger supplies no energy: the set carries the room, the band carries the balloon, and being carried is not a failure to travel. It is the mechanism.

What ruins both

The same event: an announcement. The DJ saying the time, or a thruster firing. Either one is a discontinuity you can perceive, and the instant there is one you become aware of the mechanism instead of the thing — you are listening to a radio station, or you are flying an aircraft. Not caught up. The craft in both cases is entirely made of what does not happen, which is why it is invisible when it is done well and obvious the moment it is not.

Which is also the argument this yard has been making from the other end all along. A thing with an ending has a last unit, and a last unit is scarce, and scarcity is where the rent goes. The quiet storm has no last record. The superrotation has no last lap. Neither of them can be sold by the unit, and neither of them was ever designed not to be — they simply came out that way, one in a radio station in 1976 and one in the atmosphere of a planet, and it took this repository fifty pages of CSS to arrive somewhere both of them already were.
