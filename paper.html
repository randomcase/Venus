The deck at fifty-five kilometres

Why the interesting object on Venus is a sealed biome in the cloud layer, what it costs, and the four ways it fails.

Addressed to Jed McCaleb and Mark Zuckerberg, for reasons given in the last section — briefly, one of you builds habitats and settlement rails, and the other funds science with a payback measured in decades. This document argues something that needs both. Neither has been approached about this, has any involvement in it, or has said anything about it. This is a design study written out of a working repository, not a company. Nothing has been raised and nothing is being offered. Every figure below is computed in paper.mjs and the calculation is printed beside the claim, so that disagreeing with it is a matter of checking arithmetic.

the thesisStop looking at the ground

The surface of Venus is 737 K and 92 bar and it is the reason nobody works on Venus. It is also irrelevant. At 55 kilometres the pressure is one bar, the temperature is about that of a warm afternoon, and the column of atmosphere overhead is the best radiation shield in the inner solar system that you do not have to carry.

The proposition of this paper is narrow and it is not terraforming. It is that a sealed biome — a closed ecology, some hundreds of metres across, held at that altitude — is the most interesting biological instrument available this century, and that most of what makes it hard is not what people assume.

Shielding, free

Above 55 km there are 570 g/cm² of atmosphere. Earth at sea level gives you 1033. That is 55% of the protection you have right now, at no mass cost, on a body with no magnetosphere. Radiation is not the problem. The founder bottleneck is.

Gravity you can stand in

8.71 m/s², which is 88.8% of Earth. Not a third, not a sixth. Every closed-ecology unknown about partial gravity is a question about a few per cent here, not about a different regime.

Nitrogen, already there

Venus’s atmosphere holds about 4.3× the nitrogen of Earth’s. Nitrogen is the one bulk element a sealed deck cannot manufacture and would otherwise have to ship. It is the single largest thing the site gives you for nothing.

And the day is wrong

A solar day is 116.75 Earth days against a sidereal 243.02. Nothing that photosynthesises has ever evolved under a 58-day night. This is a real problem and it is an engineering one: you light the deck, and the power budget is the honest cost of the whole programme.

first, correctlyVenus absorbs less sunlight than Earth

The usual framing is that Venus is the twin that went wrong because it sits nearer the fire. It receives 1.91× the flux, and almost every account stops there.

But Venus is the most reflective body in the inner system — a Bond albedo of 0.77 against Earth’s 0.306 — and what sets a temperature is what is absorbed, not what arrives. Venus absorbs 150 W/m². Earth absorbs 236. Venus takes in 63% of what Earth does, despite being closer, because the clouds send most of it straight back.

the inversion

227 K against 254 K

Strip both atmospheres and Venus is the colder of the two. Not one kelvin of that 737 K surface is a sunlight story. The entire difference is greenhouse: +34 K on Earth against +510 K on Venus, a factor of 15.

V: 2,601/4 × (1 − 0.77) = 150 W/m² → (150/σ)^¼ = 227 K
E: 1,361/4 × (1 − 0.306) = 236 W/m² → 254 K

This matters to an investor for one reason. The two planets did not diverge because of where they are. One of them lost control of its own atmosphere, and the deuterium-to-hydrogen ratio — roughly 150× Earth’s — is the receipt: hydrogen escapes, deuterium is twice as heavy and does not, so that enrichment is the fingerprint of an ocean split by ultraviolet with the light half gone to space. Venus had water and lost it. Which means the programme below is not about finding water there. It is about closure, and closure is the number the whole thing turns on.

lead with theseFour things that do not work

A prospectus that opens with the upside is selling. These are the four places the obvious plan dies, each with the arithmetic that kills it.

one · you cannot ship a forest

401 million years

A forest of any consequence needs on the order of 2,888 megatonnes of hydrogen — hydrogen because Venus has carbon and nitrogen in abundance and essentially no water. At a sustained and generous 7.2 tonnes a year of delivered mass, that is the figure above.

The conclusion is not that it is impossible. It is that you never ship the harvest, you ship the thing that harvests. Only the living fraction has to arrive, and the living fraction is measured in kilograms. Everything downstream in this paper follows from that one sentence.

2,888 Mt × 10⁶ t/Mt ÷ 7.2 t/yr = 401,111,111 yr

two · you cannot bombard an ocean into existence

6,618 K

Water on Venus is chemically present and thermodynamically locked. Heating the atmosphere liberates it at roughly 2.04e+15 kg per kelvin. To free one per cent of an Earth ocean you would have to raise the whole atmosphere by the figure above.

Note carefully what defeats this. It is not mass and it is not delivery — it is energy, and the plan is self-defeating on energy before any engineering question is reached. Every impact-and-volatiles scheme fails at this line.

1.35e+19 kg ÷ 2.04e+15 kg/K = 6,618 K

… but a river is not an ocean

2.2e-5 K

The same arithmetic run on a river rather than an ocean gives the number above. A river carrying 4.5e+10 kg is twenty-two millionths of a kelvin. This is the difference between a fantasy and a work order, and it is why the programme is about a deck and not a planet: everything changes by ten orders of magnitude when you stop trying to move the whole atmosphere.

4.5e+10 kg ÷ 2.04e+15 kg/K = 0.0000 K

three · you cannot dig a hurricane

2,400 km

Below the Rossby deformation radius, rotation does not organise a storm — pressure gradients simply relax and nothing spins up. On Venus that radius is the figure above, because the planet turns so slowly.

The confirmation was not arranged and is the reason to believe it: the only rotating structures Venus actually has are the polar vortices, and they are about 2,000 km across. The planet agrees with the calculation. You can dig a dust bowl. You cannot dig a hurricane. Anything in the programme that wanted a self-organising storm system is deleted.

four · the nutrients are there and not available

98.8% locked

Residence time, not inventory, is what a closed system runs on. At any instant roughly 1.2% of a site’s phosphorus is in a form anything can take up; the rest is in transit through mineral and biological pools. A sealed deck that sizes its nutrient budget on total inventory is over-provisioned on paper and starving in fact.

This is the failure mode that has ended every closed-ecology experiment on record, and it is invisible for the first two years, which is exactly how long the stored fraction lasts.

the findingWeight falls, inertia does not

Here is the thing about 88.8% gravity that is not in the brochures, and it is the most useful single fact in this document.

Weight scales. Mass does not. A grain of silt on the deck weighs 88.8% of what it weighs here, and has exactly 100% of the inertia. Settling velocity goes with weight; resistance to being moved goes with mass. So the same current carries the same grain 12.6% further before it drops, and it falls 11.2% slower when it does.

Sediment sorting, delta formation, soil development and every nutrient-transport process in a water column are all functions of exactly that ratio. The deck is not a slightly weaker Earth. It is a place where fluids build landscape measurably better, and it is the only environment we can reach where that is true.

That is the scientific case in one paragraph, and it does not depend on anybody settling anywhere. It is a soil-physics experiment that cannot be run on Earth, cannot be run in orbit, and cannot be run on Mars — Mars is 38% and that is a different regime, not a control.

getting thereSix months is a choice, not a constraint

The 146-day figure everybody quotes is the minimum-energy Hohmann transfer. It is the cheapest trajectory and therefore the slowest one, and treating it as the transit time is a category error — it is the transit time if you are optimising for propellant and nothing else.

Drop the transfer orbit’s perihelion below Venus and you arrive sooner. The whole curve, computed from the vis-viva equation and Kepler’s equation:

The shape of that table is the argument. Going from 146 days to 95 costs a departure C3 of 13.9 against 6.2 — real, and ordinary. Going to 52 days costs 141.6, which is a different vehicle.

And the arrival column is where Venus quietly wins. You arrive faster on every fast trajectory, and Venus has an atmosphere thick enough to take that velocity out of you for free. On Mars a fast transfer is punished twice, because the arrival burn scales too and there is not enough air to help. Here it is punished once. The relay wants short crossings, and this is the one destination where short crossings are affordable.

Light delay at arrival is 4.94 minutes one way, so a question and its answer is under ten. Nothing on the deck can be run by anybody on Earth in real time, and nothing needs to be.

the highest-leverage numberRecovery ratio, and what it is worth

A crew of 20 moves about 500 kg a day through the water loop. Circulation is free. What is not free is the fraction the loop fails to return, and make-up mass goes as (1 − r) — a reciprocal, so all the leverage sits at the top end where it is least visible.

the lever

20× less mass

From 1.9 percentage points of recovery. Nothing available in propulsion comes near that, and unlike propulsion it is bench work that can be finished on Earth before a vehicle is chosen. If one number in this programme deserves a dedicated team, it is this one, and it is the cheapest thing in the document.

The reserve tank is the same argument in reverse. A missed window costs 730 days — 584 to the next alignment plus 146 in transit. At 95% recovery a thirty-tonne tank, an entire shipment, buys about 1,200 days. At 99.9% a five-tonne tank buys twenty-seven years. Recovery ratio is worth more per kilogram than anything else on the manifest.

who does itNine, and one button

The unit is a core of nine: one sergeant, two corporals, six auxiliary with communications among them rather than beside them. A core that carries its own signaller reports without asking anybody’s leave; a core that borrows one has a choke point inside it before it leaves the ground.

Two cores, a general, and a mobile rear guard who commands nobody: twenty people. Three earlier arrangements were costed at 38 and 56 and rejected, because all three are the same depth from the top to the furthest hand — the extra people bought width and no reach.

The command graph was then run through a cut-vertex routine, and the result is the reason for the operating rule: every officer is a choke point, without exception, because a tree has no second path by construction. A structure in which every node is a choke point cannot be handed live judgement calls. So the relay gets one start and one shutdown and nothing in between. Discretion is spent once, when the order is written, and after that it is gone. That is not a convenience. It is the whole safety argument.

how it settlesThree claims about the ledger

A programme with a hundred-year payback and no product needs a settlement layer that does not depend on anybody’s continued goodwill. Three positions, each of which is a technical statement rather than a preference.

Decentralisation is about reads, not copies

A system is decentralised exactly to the extent that no component’s behaviour is a function of another component’s state. A thousand mirrors of one authoritative list is a centralised system with good uptime. The useful question about any instrument is not how many copies exist but how many reads it performs and who can write to each one.

Fungibility is destroyed by memory and nothing else

Two units are interchangeable exactly as long as nothing anybody can look up distinguishes them. A ledger storing only balances is fungible; one storing provenance has made them different objects wearing the same denomination, and somebody will price the difference. This is why an openly auditable chain is less fungible than a banknote, not more.

The middle hop is the only one that matters

Business to peer, peer to peer, peer to business. The middle hop is the entire difference from every other arrangement, and whether it is real is a graph question with a computable answer: is there any single party whose removal disconnects issuer from redeemer? If yes, that party holds a discretion, and a discretion is where extraction attaches. It has to be plural at both ends or the choke point has only moved somewhere harder to see.

The working implementation issues one non-transferable token per notebook, minted client-side, never leaving the browser. It is deliberately the least fungible object in the system because it carries provenance — which is the point. It is not money and would be poor money. It signs authorship so that when the best version of something is copied, the copy still says whose it was.

the addressWhy this document has two names on it

Because the programme needs two things that almost never sit in the same balance sheet, and each of you has publicly built one of them.

Jed McCaleb. Vast is a company whose actual product is people living in a sealed volume for a long time, which is the same engineering problem as the deck with a different sky outside it. Separately, Stellar and Ripple are settlement systems built on the position that the middle hop should not require permission. This paper argues that a century-long biological programme needs both a habitat that works and a ledger nobody can switch off, and those are unusually the same person’s two careers.

Mark Zuckerberg. The Chan Zuckerberg Initiative funds basic biology on timescales where the return arrives after the funder is finished caring, and Meta has run infrastructure at a scale where the interesting failures are emergent rather than component-level. A sealed biome is exactly that: a systems problem in which every individual part is understood and the assembly is not, and where the honest deliverable for the first decade is measurements that mean nothing yet.

Neither of you has been contacted about this and neither has said anything about it. The names are here because the argument genuinely needs both halves, and naming who builds each half is more useful than addressing nobody.

what is actually being asked for

Not a rocket. The programme’s first decade needs five things and none of them leaves the ground:

  1. A closed water loop pushed from 98% to 99.9% recovery. On a bench, here, for years, with the assay running the whole time. It is the highest-leverage number in the programme by a factor of 20 and the cheapest thing on this list, and no launch has to happen for it to be worth doing.
  2. A 88.8% gravity sediment column. Centrifuge, on Earth, running for years. Settling and transport at this exact fraction, measured rather than modelled. This is the cheapest experiment in the document and the one the whole soil case rests on.
  3. A residence-time study in a genuinely sealed system. Not inventory — availability, tracked continuously, for longer than the two years it takes the stored fraction to run out and the failure to become visible.
  4. A mycorrhizal assembly trial under a 58-day night. Whether a soil network establishes from a culture collection under that light cycle at that gravity, and if it does not, which of the two is the reason.
  5. An unconditional line for the people doing it. No milestones, no deliverables, no review. The moment the stream is conditional it is leverage, and leverage is a discretion, and every extractive relationship traces back to somebody having a choice about somebody else’s income. The output is the expense. That is what patronage means and it is the only funding shape this work can take.

Every one of those is falsifiable inside ten years, on Earth, for less than the cost of a single launch.

the honest closeWhat would show this wrong

The centrifuge column returns null — sorting at 88.8% is indistinguishable from sorting at 100% within measurement error. That kills the soil-physics argument outright and most of the scientific case with it, and it is a cheap experiment specifically so that it can kill the expensive ones early.

Or the lighting budget for a 116.75-day cycle turns out to dominate everything else so completely that the deck is a power station with a garden attached, at which point the honest thing is to say so and go and do something else.

Both of those are findable before anybody buys a rocket, which is the strongest thing this paper can claim for itself.
