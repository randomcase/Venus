There is no fragment in the address, so this page does not know where you are. That is the correct state for a first visit and it is selected for deliberately — .school:not(:has(:target)) — rather than being left as whatever happens.

Start at unit 0. It is short and it exists so that nobody starts at unit 3, reads a compound lookup table, and concludes this is harder than it is. If you have written CSS professionally for years, unit 0 is still where to start: the constraint that reorganises everything here is not difficult, it is just unfamiliar, and it is stated once in unit 0 and assumed everywhere after.

Nothing on this page is timed and no unit claims a duration. Those numbers are invented wherever you see them. What each unit has instead is a test — a thing you can do or cannot do — and that is the only honest way to know you are finished.

unit 0Cold startthe one constraint

What these pages are, and the single rule that shapes every one of them: a counter can only be printed after the things that increment it. Inputs first in the DOM, totals after, and the layout put back with grid. Every board in the yard is arranged around that and nothing else.

Read

  • bare.html — all five techniques in 105 lines. Read the whole file. It is short enough to hold in your head at once, and nothing later is more than an elaboration of it.
  • whale.html — the same five with a working example each. Skim now, return per unit.

Exercise

Take template.html, move the totals block above the inputs, and open it. Note what the numbers do. Put it back. You have now met the constraint the hard way, which is the only way it stays met.

You are done with unit 0 when

You can say, without looking, why the totals are at the bottom of the source on every page in this yard and why moving them is not a style decision.

unit 1Counters are arithmetictechnique one

counter-reset on body, counter-increment on the label next to a checked input, content:counter(x) to print. The browser adds for you. It does not subtract, multiply, divide or take a minimum, and the whole of unit 3 exists because of that sentence.

Read

  • station.html — a docket that sums itself across ten classes and eleven mounts.
  • auxiliaries.html — twelve hulls, each declaring its own numbers on the line where you select it.

Exercise

Build a board with six checkboxes and three counters, where every box contributes to at least two of them and the numbers are written on the labels. Then remove one number from a label and find where the total goes wrong. There is no hidden model to check it against, which is the point.

You are done with unit 1 when

Someone can point at any number on your board and you can point at the line of markup that produced it.

unit 2:has() is statetechnique two

An ancestor styled by what is inside it. That is a state machine with nothing to keep in sync, and the most common way to get it wrong is scope: the rule must be anchored on an ancestor of both the switch and the thing it drives. A rule scoped to the row will never reach an element in another subtree, it will fail silently, and it will look like the property is unsupported.

Read

  • cruise-control.html — one control over eight hulls, with four stations taking a tariff as they pass.
  • personnel.html — gating, shortfalls, and a return that only appears when it should.
  • learn.html, mistake one — read it now rather than after you have made it.

Exercise

Gate one control behind another: a second group of options that does not appear until the first is set. Then deliberately scope the rule to the wrong ancestor and watch nothing happen — no error, no warning, nothing. That silence is what you are learning to recognise.

You are done with unit 2 when

Given a rule that does not fire, scope is the first thing you check and you are usually right.

unit 3Compound :has() is a lookuptechnique three

:has(A):has(B) keyed on a pair. This is how you get multiplication out of a language that has none: you write the products. Three options by two states is six rules and there is no shortcut — but the table is visible and every cell was typed by someone who can be asked about it, which is a better property than most computed tables have.

Read

  • court.html — findings of fact that only mean something in pairs.
  • pulse.html — the Underwriter reads two keys; the Clerk reads two more. Both tables are printed on the page so the rules can be checked against them.
  • bench.html — Fern's six cells, and the opinion assignment, which is three keys deep.

Exercise

Write a 3 × 2 lookup: six rules, six answers, and the table printed on the page next to it. Then add a fourth option to the first axis and notice you owe two more rules. Pay them. The moment you find yourself wanting to avoid that cost is the moment to re-read what the cost is buying.

You are done with unit 3 when

You can look at a page and say how many rules its widest lookup costs, and you no longer think of that number as a problem to be solved.

unit 4Cascade order is authoritytechnique four

Later rules win over equally specific ones above them. So the thing that must not be overridden goes last — and if that sentence sounds fragile, it is, and it is also the entire enforcement mechanism in every page here that has a stop rule. Knowing that it is the entire mechanism is the unit.

Read

  • pulse.html — the seal at the bottom of the stylesheet: the tally is correct before it and is not a decision until after it. Also the Chair's silence, which beats two rules three lines above it on position alone.
  • bench.html — and the note recording a stop rule that was removed when it became unreachable, in place and conspicuously, rather than left in to look careful.

Exercise

Put a stop rule at the bottom of one of your own boards, confirm it works, then move it up three lines. The board will keep counting, perfectly consistently, and will now be wrong. Leave it broken for a minute and look at it, because that is what this failure looks like in production and nothing announces it.

You are done with unit 4 when

You can find every rule in a stylesheet that is load-bearing on position, and you write a comment above each one saying so.

unit 5Transport, and data that is not codetechniques five and six

<form method="get"> — the browser serialises the checked inputs and navigates. No fetch, no endpoint, no server. form="vy" associates an input with a form anywhere in the document, so your markup is not reorganised around the transport. And <script type="application/json"> is stored and never executed: data that cannot become code.

Read

  • cargo.html and contract.html — a document authored on one page and read on another.
  • bridge.html — one of the two pages in the yard that runs script, and marked as such everywhere it is linked. Read why it needs to.

Exercise

Give a board a form="vy" button that opens another page with the state in the query string. Then try to read that state back in CSS on the receiving page. You cannot. Sit with that for a moment before you go to unit 6, because unit 6 is the answer and it is only interesting if you have felt the problem.

You are done with unit 5 when

You can say precisely which half of a URL a stylesheet can read, and what the other half is for.

unit 6The persistence layerthe sixth technique

The fragment is the only durable state a scriptless page has, and :target is the only selector that reads it. A link writes it, the browser keeps it in the URL and in history, and :has() acts on it. Reload restores it, a bookmark is a save file, and Back is undo. The price: one target per document, so a stored value must name a whole combination and there is no partial update.

Read

  • persist.html — the layer, its four lines, its cold-start case, and its limits stated where the temptation to imply otherwise would be.
  • This page. Your place in the course is a fragment; that is the technique being used on you while it is explained.

Exercise

Give one of your boards a URL state: anchors, links, and a cold-start panel for the case where there is no fragment at all. Then reload it, bookmark it, send it to someone, and press Back. Four confirmations, none of them script.

You are done with unit 6 when

You can state the cost of adding a third axis to a stored state without having to work it out, and you reach for fewer axes rather than a grid with unreachable cells in it.

unit 7The ethicthe unit you can fail while everything works

This one is not a technique and there is no API for it. No number on a page may be one the page did not compute. If you cannot compute it, print the parts and say why the difference is absent. Where a comparison has to be made by hand, enumerate it and print the counters that let a reader audit your enumeration. When a stop rule becomes unreachable, remove it and say so where it was. When one person holds two seats, put it on the card.

Read

  • bench.html — sixteen judges, twelve circuits, every outcome computed by hand and every counter on the page there so you can check the hand.
  • chamber.html — a board where the thing being priced never appears in the arithmetic, said out loud.
  • exhibit.html — a record entered uncorrected, with the findings tied to line numbers so anyone who disagrees can name the line.

Exercise

Take your best board and find the number on it that the page did not actually compute. There is one. Either compute it or replace it with the parts and a sentence about why the difference is absent. Then find the thing your board is quietly not showing, and show it.

You are done with unit 7 when

Somebody can ask where any figure came from and the answer is never “it looked about right”.
