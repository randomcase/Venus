Nickelodeon against Disney

a discovery

A discovery rather than an opinion for one reason: the tests already existed and were built for other things. The serial test came from 544 luxury houses. The choke test came from lesson two. The +1 law came from the arcade. None of them were written with a cartoon studio in mind, which is what makes their answers worth having.

Disney

founded 1923 · Burbank

the model

IP first. The property is the asset and the maker is staff.

whose name is on it

The studio. For decades the animators on the classic features were largely uncredited on screen, and the name on the picture was Walt Disney’s.

the term

Copyright on a 1928 work runs 95 years, so Steamboat Willie entered the US public domain on 1 January 2024. The 1998 term extension that held it there that long is so associated with this company that it is commonly nicknamed the Mickey Mouse Protection Act.

Nickelodeon

founded 1979 · New York, then Burbank

the model

Creator first. The maker is the asset and the property follows them.

whose name is on it

The animator, by name, on screen. The 1991 Nicktoons launch — Doug, Rugrats, Ren & Stimpy — handed creative control to individual creators at a point when television animation was largely anonymous studio work, and that was the deliberate difference.

the term

Corporately owned like everything else, and nothing here is a cooperative. The difference is not who holds the copyright. It is who the audience believes made it.

2024
the year Steamboat Willie stopped being property

The discovery is the handoff. Copyright expires. Trademark does not, so long as the mark stays in use. A 1928 work carries a 95-year term, so Steamboat Willie entered the US public domain on 1 January 2024 — and on that day the cartoon became copyable by anybody while the mouse did not, because Mickey is a live trademark and trademarks renew indefinitely.

The protection was moved off the thing with a clock on it and onto the thing without one. That is not a loophole; it is two different laws doing two different jobs. But the effect is a ledger that never lapses — and by lesson three, a ledger that never lapses is a good that can never become fungible. Disney did not out-compete the public domain. It stepped off the axis the public domain runs on.

copyright — 95 years, expired
trademark — still running

the three tests none of them written for this

Does the ledger remember? Memory is the only thing that destroys fungibility.
the serial test — from houses.html
Disney

Maximally remembered. Every character is dated, registered, defended, and its lineage is a matter of legal record. There is no such thing as a generic Disney property — that is the entire design.

Nickelodeon

Weakly remembered as a catalogue and strongly remembered as a set of people. Ask who made SpongeBob and the answer is Stephen Hillenburg, a marine biologist. Ask who made Cinderella and the answer is Disney. Both are corporate properties; only one of them has a person attached in the public record.

Remove one party. Does the rest disconnect?
the choke test — from lesson two
Disney

One owner for the whole catalogue. Remove the company and nothing flows, because nothing was ever anywhere else. A textbook cut vertex, and deliberately so.

Nickelodeon

Structurally the same single owner — and this is where the test stops flattering the creator model. The corporate cut vertex is identical. What differs is that the CREATOR is a second node, and a second node is the difference between a monopoly and a graph.

Does it ever end? Every option is +1 and nothing is retired.
the +1 law — from the arcade
Disney

The franchise never ends. Sequels, parks, remakes, a live-action pass over the animated one — every option is +1 and nothing is ever retired. The yard’s idle-game law, run as a business for a century.

Nickelodeon

Also never ends, and the failure mode shows: the properties that outlived their creators kept producing, because the ownership was never really with the person whose name was on it.

Disney what the tests actually say

THE DISCOVERY IS THE HANDOFF. Copyright expires; trademark does not, as long as you keep using the mark in commerce. When Steamboat Willie fell into the public domain in 2024, the 1928 CARTOON became copyable by anybody — and the MOUSE did not, because Mickey is a live trademark and trademarks renew forever.

So the protection was moved off the thing with a clock on it and onto the thing without one. That is not a loophole; it is two different laws doing two different jobs. But the effect is a ledger that never lapses, and by this yard’s own lesson three, a ledger that never lapses is a good that can never become fungible.

Nickelodeon what the tests actually say

AND THE COUNTER-DISCOVERY. The creator model looks like decentralisation and is not. Ownership sat in the same place the whole time; what was distributed was CREDIT, which is cheap to give and does not move a single legal right.

That is worth saying plainly because it is the most common shape of fake decentralisation there is: attribution handed out generously while control stays exactly where it was. The yard’s syndication entry already names this — a lone party is a monopoly whatever it calls itself, and a second name on the poster is not a second party to the contract.

and the honest part where the comparison stops flattering anybody

Run the choke test properly and both come back the same. One corporate owner each, one cut vertex each. The creator model distributed credit, which is cheap to give and moves no legal right, and this yard’s syndication entry already says what that is: a lone party is a monopoly whatever it calls itself, and a second name on the poster is not a second party to the contract.
The 56 years between them are not the interesting gap either. Both never end, both are owned in one place, and both obey the +1 law. What separates them is a single legal decision about which register to keep the value in — and that decision, not the cartoons, is why one of these catalogues is worth what it is worth.
How to falsify the whole page. Find a property whose copyright lapsed and whose commercial value did NOT move to a trademark or a later derivative. If the value simply evaporated on schedule, the handoff is not a strategy, it is an artefact of picking the most famous example.
