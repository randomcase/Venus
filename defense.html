Cloud Deck Defence

Fifty-three kilometres above the surface, four hazards inbound and three mounts to answer them with. Something gets through — the question is which. no script †

The wave

01 · aerosol Sulfuric acid

The cloud deck is acid droplets. Bare aluminium and ordinary polymer do not last.

02 · shear Superrotation

The atmosphere circles the planet in four days — about 100 m/s at this altitude, and the gusts are the problem, not the mean.

03 · thermal Descent soak

Sink below fifty kilometres and it is 75 °C and climbing. Losing altitude is a heat problem before it is anything else.

04 · flux Ultraviolet

Above most of the cloud the solar flux is fierce, and it is what ages an envelope that acid has not already taken.

Cost is counted; the allowance is printed next to it and the comparison is yours to make. Three mounts against four hazards means at least one is getting through — a defence you can fully solve is a puzzle, and a puzzle teaches nothing about triage.

The wave is already inbound. Placement closes when you commit, and there is no undo. Committed. It went how it went.

The wave as it passed

Sulfuric acid. Answered only by the fluoropolymer skin. Through, it takes the envelope slowly and you will not notice for months.
Superrotation shear. Answered only by the drogue. Through, she yaws and the rigging works against itself all the way round the planet.
Descent soak. Answered by either the ballonet or the radiator — two different ways to solve one problem, and you only need one.
Ultraviolet. Answered only by the shade, and the cheapest counter on the board at twenty. Easy to leave out, and it is the one that ages everything else.
A patch drone is mounted. It answers envelope creep, which is not in this wave. It was the cheapest thing on the list and it did nothing — the classic tower-defence mistake, which is buying coverage against the threat you can afford rather than the one that is coming.
Both thermal counters are mounted. The ballonet and the radiator solve the same hazard by different means, so the second one bought nothing this wave. Redundancy is a real strategy and it is not free — it cost you a mount that had a hazard with no answer.

There is no score. Four lines, and each says stopped or through. What that is worth depends on which one you chose to let past, and the page will not pretend to know that better than you do.

Using this as a template

Change three lists and it is a different game. Everything else is layout.

ChangeWhere
The wave — how many hazards, what they are four here; more makes it harder without changing a rule the .wave block, and one .res row per hazard
The defences — the options in every mount keep them identical across mounts or placement stops meaning anything the three .mount blocks, and the cost counters
The coverage map — which defence answers which hazard the only interesting rule on the page three selectors per defence, one per mount, setting the matching .r-* row to STOPPED
The pressure — mounts fewer than hazards this is the design, not the difficulty add or remove a .mount

What cannot be added without script. Waves over time, damage that accumulates, towers that fire at what is nearest, anything where the outcome depends on when rather than what. CSS has no loop and no clock it can read. If you want that, this page is the planning screen in front of it — and the planning screen is the part players actually replay.
