The atelier

what survives the deck

The houses lists 544 of them and what each one makes. This asks the one question that turns that into a Venus layer rather than an Earth one: does the material survive at 55 km. The deck sits inside the cloud layer, the cloud aerosol is concentrated sulfuric acid, and so the answer is chemistry rather than taste.

Of 544 houses, 0 work in a material that survives outside on the deck.

Not a rhetorical zero — a counted one. Sulfuric acid dehydrates cellulose, so cotton, linen, viscose and lyocell convert to carbon. It hydrolyses the amide bond, so nylon and aramid go. It hydrolyses collagen, so leather goes, and leather is the most valuable material in the houses layer. Hermès, Zimmerli, Loro Piana, Charvet, every Naples tailor and every Northampton laster: all of them make garments that would not last the walk.

87 houses work only in crafts this board has no fibre mapping for — beauty, retail, gunmaking — and are counted as neither.
And then the reason that does not end the subject.

At 55 km the pressure is near half a bar and the temperature is near room temperature. Inside a habitation at that altitude you need no suit, no mask and no heating — which makes it the only place off Earth where an ordinary shirt is the correct garment. So the wardrobe splits absolutely, and the split is the design brief. Outside is fluoropolymer and nothing else. Inside is the whole of Earth’s wardrobe, worn at 0.888 g.
4inert
PTFEfluoropolymer · spinnable

Chemically inert to sulfuric acid at every concentration and temperature the deck offers. Spinnable into fibre and castable into membrane. This is the answer, and it has been the answer since 1938.

It drapes badly, it does not dye, and it costs. All three are design problems.

worn as: Gore, industrial membrane

FEP and PFAfluoropolymer

Melt-processable fluoropolymers — they can be extruded as film where PTFE cannot. Inert. This is what a deck garment is laminated with.

worn as: film, lining, window glazing

ECTFE and PVDFfluoropolymer · spinnable

Inert to sulfuric, tougher and stiffer than PTFE, and weldable.

worn as: Halar, Kynar; chemical plant

FKM (Viton)elastomer

The elastomer question, which every garment has: a suit is only as good as its wrists, neck and ankles. FKM is the seal that holds where the fabric meets the body.

worn as: seals, gaskets, cuffs

5good
Polypropylenepolyolefin · spinnable

A genuinely acid-resistant fibre that can be spun, woven and worn, and the cheapest thing on this list that could go outside. Its limit is heat, not chemistry.

worn as: industrial felt, filtration, base layers

Butyl rubberelastomer

Good acid resistance and very low gas permeability. The glove material.

worn as: gloves, bladders

Glass fibremineral · spinnable

E-glass is attacked by acid; C-glass and ECR are formulated against it. The distinction matters and is the kind of thing a specification gets wrong once.

worn as: industrial cloth

Basalt fibremineral · spinnable

Spun from melted rock, better in acid than E-glass, and made from the commonest rock on the Venusian surface. The one material on this list that could in principle be sourced there.

worn as: industrial cloth

Carbon fibremineral · spinnable

Chemically excellent, mechanically a structure rather than a garment.

worn as: structure, not clothing

3fair
Polyesterpolyester · spinnable

PET resists dilute acid well and concentrated sulfuric poorly. Better than anything above it and not good enough for the deck.

worn as: most of what is actually worn

Acrylicvinyl · spinnable

Decent acid resistance; poor everything else.

worn as: knitwear substitutes

PBIpolymer · spinnable

Outstanding thermally, only fair in strong acid. Again: heat is not acid.

worn as: firefighting, flight suits

4poor
Woolprotein · spinnable

The interesting one. Wool is CARBONISED with sulfuric acid as a routine industrial step: the bath destroys the burrs and vegetable matter in a fleece and leaves the keratin. That is a direct demonstration that wool outlasts cellulose in the same acid.

It is not a survival claim. Carbonising is dilute, warm and brief. Cloud aerosol is 75 to 98 per cent and permanent, and keratin does go in that. Wool is in a better class, not a safe one.

worn as: tailoring, knitwear, every coat

Cashmereprotein · spinnable

Keratin, finer. Same chemistry as wool and less mass to lose.

worn as: Loro Piana, Barrie, Johnstons, Begg

Vicuñaprotein · spinnable

The most expensive fibre on Earth, and keratin like the rest.

worn as: Loro Piana

Aramidpolyamide · spinnable

Aromatic polyamide. Excellent against heat and cutting, and still an amide — strong sulfuric attacks it, and concentrated sulfuric dissolves it outright. Fire protection is not acid protection.

worn as: Nomex, Kevlar; protective wear

10destroyed
Cottoncellulose · spinnable

Concentrated sulfuric acid dehydrates cellulose. The reaction is the classroom demonstration — sugar to a black carbon column — and cotton is cellulose. It does not weaken; it converts.

Sea Island cotton is the top of the men’s market and it is still cellulose.

worn as: most of the yard

Linencellulose · spinnable

Flax is cellulose. Same reaction, same result.

worn as: shirting, summer tailoring

Hempcellulose · spinnable

Cellulose.

worn as: canvas, workwear

Viscose and rayoncellulose · spinnable

Regenerated cellulose, and regenerating it does not change what it is. Acid takes it faster than cotton because the fibre is weaker wet.

worn as: lining, drape, most fluid dresses

Lyocellcellulose · spinnable

Cellulose, solvent-spun. The solvent is the innovation; the chemistry is not.

worn as: CDLP and most of the new underwear

Silkprotein · spinnable

Fibroin is a protein but not a robust one — acid hydrolyses the peptide backbone and silk loses strength fast. Weaker in acid than wool, which is the reverse of how the two are usually ranked.

worn as: every lingerie house, Hermès scarves

Leatherprotein

Collagen, and tanned collagen at that. Acid strips the tannage and hydrolyses the protein. The single most valuable material in the houses layer and one of the least survivable.

worn as: Hermès, Vuitton, Delvaux, every bag

Nylonpolyamide · spinnable

Polyamide, and the amide bond is exactly what acid hydrolyses. Nylon is specifically poor against sulfuric — worse than the plant fibres people assume are the fragile ones.

worn as: Prada’s nylon, outerwear, hosiery

Natural latexelastomer

Polyisoprene. Concentrated sulfuric attacks the double bonds in the backbone directly — natural rubber hardens, cracks and fails. It is worth listing separately from butyl because the two are constantly confused: butyl is synthetic, saturated, and holds; latex is neither.

A garment material with a real couture tradition and no future outside.

worn as: Atsuko Kudo; latex couture

Elastanepolyurethane · spinnable

Segmented polyurethane. The urethane linkage hydrolyses in acid, and the soft segments it joins are polyether or polyester, which go too. It fails faster than whatever fibre it is blended with.

THE POINT IS THE BLEND. Elastane is rarely more than two to five per cent of a garment by weight and it is load-bearing for fit — take it out and the piece no longer holds a shape. So a polyester-elastane knit does not survive at polyester’s rating. It survives at elastane’s. The weakest fibre in a blend sets the rating, not the majority one, which means the fair band on this board reads better than any real garment does: almost nothing modern is unblended.

worn as: the stretch in almost everything — lingerie, hosiery, swim, sportswear, and most modern tailoring

drape is gravity and gravity is different

A hanging length of cloth is a pendulum, and a pendulum’s period goes as 1/√g. Nothing about the cloth changes on the deck. The rate at which it moves does, by a factor that depends on nothing but the two gravities.

a shirt tail1.00s1.06s+6.1%
a jacket skirt1.35s1.43s+6.1%

The last column is constant, because the length cancels: √(9.807/8.71) = 1.0611. Every hem on Venus swings 6.1% slower than the same hem on Earth, whatever it is cut from and however long it is. A bias-cut gown, a pleated skirt, a cape, a veil — all of them settle later, and a cutter who has only ever worked at 9.807 will get the timing wrong before they get anything else wrong.

Basalt is the one to notice. It is spun from melted rock, it beats E-glass in acid, and it is made from the commonest rock on the Venusian surface. Every other material on this list has to be shipped. That is the difference between a supply line and an industry.
A garment is also its openings. Fabric that survives and cuffs that do not is a suit that fails at the wrists, which is why FKM is on the fabric list at all despite not being a fabric.

the blend floor a garment is its weakest fibre

Nothing here is worn unblended. Elastane is two to five per cent of almost every modern garment by weight and it is load-bearing for fit — take it out and the piece stops holding a shape. It is also polyurethane, and the urethane linkage hydrolyses. So a garment rates as the worst of its fibres, never the majority one:

Which means the bands on the left read better than any real garment does. Polyester is fair; a polyester jersey is not. The one line that survives the blend is the last: a fluoropolymer shell laminated to a carrier fails at the carrier, so a deck garment has to be fluoropolymer through — seams, thread and all — or it is only as good as its worst thread.

the craft mapping stated, so it can be argued with

The zero above is only as good as the map from a craft to the fibres it implies. That map is written out in atelier.mjs rather than inferred, so it can be checked and disputed. Crafts with no mapping are counted as neither, not as a pass: beauty, crystal, eyewear, gunmaking, home, jewelry, lighters, manufacturing, pens, porcelain, pâtisserie, resale.
