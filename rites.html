18 entries, Bronze Age to 1843, and every one declares what class of evidence it rests on — because the honest answer changes completely across three thousand years. That field is the difference between a dance somebody is still being taught and a dance somebody reconstructed from a painting.

The early rites are disguise. Be unreadable to whatever is abroad when the harvest is in. The medieval ones are noise — bells, masks, volume, aimed at nothing in particular. After the Black Death one appears that concedes: the Danse Macabre wards off nothing and says so.

And then in the coldest decades of the Little Ice Age the thing being warded off acquires names and addresses, and the mechanism becomes prohibition aimed at neighbours. The rites do not get more effective. They get more specific about who to blame, and the timing tracks the temperature.

Evidence class, counted. Physical means an object you can date — antlers, bells, a painted wall. Continuous means it is still performed by people who were taught it rather than by people who read about it. Those two are worth far more than the rest and there are fewer of them than anybody expects.

the entries in order

The figures with their arms up

unknownphysical

Tanum, Bohuslän · c. 1700–500 BCE · Bronze Age · against: unknown

Rock carvings of processions, ships, lur-players and figures with raised arms, alongside bronze lurs deposited in pairs in bogs and the Trundholm sun chariot of about 1400 BCE.

This entry exists to hold the silence open. We have the horns and we have the raised arms. We do not have the steps, the occasion, or the reason, and every confident account of Bronze Age harvest dance is filling that gap with something invented.

Guising at Samhain

disguisetextual

Ireland and Scotland · 1 November · Iron Age into modern · against: whatever is abroad when the harvest is in

The harvest is gathered, the year turns, and the boundary is open. People go out masked and in costume.

The mechanism is NOT driving anything off. It is being unreadable to it. That is a different theory of danger from the one the later noise rites run on, and it is the older of the two.

Lughnasadh

prohibitiontextual

Ireland · 1 August · Iron Age · against: eating the crop before it is in

The first-fruits assembly, with games and contests at Tailtiu, and temporary marriages contracted for the duration.

A first-fruits rite is a prohibition with a party attached. Nobody eats the new crop until the day, which stops it being eaten before the harvest is safely in — enforceable in a way a rule is not.

The Abbots Bromley Horn Dance

processionphysical

Staffordshire · September · medieval into continuous · against: not stated by anyone who performs it

Six sets of reindeer antlers carried through the parish, with a hobby horse, a fool, and a bowman.

The only English folk dance with a datable object at the centre of it: the antlers carbon-date to around 1065, and there had been no reindeer in Britain for a very long time before that. The dance may be younger than the horns; the horns are not in doubt.

The Perchtenlauf

noisecontinuous

Tyrol and Salzburg · midwinter · medieval into continuous · against: winter, and what comes with it

Masked runners in carved wooden faces and heavy cowbells, moving through the village to drive the season out. Krampus belongs to the same family.

Weight and volume are the tools. The bells are not accompaniment — they ARE the rite, and the masks are so the wearer is not the one doing it.

Ringing against the storm

noisephysical

across Europe · whenever the sky darkened · medieval to 18th century · against: hail and lightning on the standing crop

Church bells rung to break up storms, many of them cast with the inscription FULGURA FRANGO — I break the lightning.

The most institutional version of the whole subject, and the one with a body count. Ringers were killed in towers by the strikes they were ringing against, and the practice was eventually restricted for that reason. A wet rope in a stone tower under a thunderhead is a lightning conductor with a person attached.

Beating the bounds

processioncontinuous

England · Rogationtide · medieval into continuous · against: blight, and forgetting where the parish ends

The parish walks its own boundary, blessing the crops, and children were beaten against the boundary stones.

Two jobs in one walk: the fields are blessed and the map is put into somebody’s body. Memory enforced through pain, because a child who was hit at that stone will still know where it is in fifty years.

The bird-scarer

substitutiontextual

England · sowing to harvest · medieval into modern · against: rooks and pigeons, which are not demons

Boys employed to stand in the field all day with wooden clappers. The stuffed figure and the word scarecrow are sixteenth-century.

The scarecrow is person-shaped because it replaced a person. It is the only entry here aimed at an animal rather than a spirit, and it is the only one that reliably worked.

The Danse Macabre

concessionphysical

Paris, then everywhere · from the 1420s · after the Black Death · against: nothing — that is the point

Painted at the Cimetière des Innocents in 1424–25, then Basel, Lübeck, and Holbein’s woodcuts in the 1520s. Death leads everyone away by the hand, in order of rank, beginning with the pope.

The form flips here. Every rite above wards something off; this one concedes that nothing can be warded off, and it arrives one generation after the plague. When protection stops being believable the dance does not stop — it changes what it is for.

The dancing manias

nonetextual

Aachen, Strasbourg · 1374 and 1518 · late medieval · against: not applicable

People danced for days, in numbers, some until they collapsed or died. Strasbourg 1518 followed a stretch of famine and disease.

The only entry here nobody organised. It belongs in a harvest layer because of what may have been in the bread.

Ergot

nonephysical

wherever rye grew wet · any bad year · all of them · against: not applicable

A fungus on rye. Eating the bread causes writhing, burning limbs, visions, and gangrene — St Anthony’s Fire.

The demon in the field was real for at least some of this, and it was a fungus growing on the grain. A wet harvest did not merely fail. It could poison the people who ate it into exactly the behaviour the other entries were trying to explain.
what is disputedThe mechanism is certain: Claviceps purpurea produces alkaloids that cause convulsions, hallucination and gangrene, and wet years favour it. What is NOT settled is whether it explains the dancing manias or the Salem accusations — that application is much contested and probably over-used. This entry asserts the fungus and not the explanation.

Weather magic

prohibitiontextual

Franconia and beyond · peak 1580–1630 · the Little Ice Age · against: named neighbours

The standard witchcraft accusation was causing hail, frost and blight. A catastrophic late frost across Franconia on 27 May 1626 destroyed the crop; the Bamberg and Würzburg mass trials follow within months and run to 1631.

THE DARK TURN, AND IT IS THE POINT OF THE LAYER. The rites above aim at nothing in particular — weather, winter, the dark. When the climate deteriorated and the harvests failed repeatedly, the thing being warded off acquired names and addresses. The persecution peak tracks the coldest decades.

The ring shout

disguisecontinuous

the Sea Islands and the coastal South · from the 18th century · under enslavement, and continuous · against: the prohibition itself

A counterclockwise circle, call and response, hand-clapping and a stick beaten on the floor. The feet never cross and never fully lift.

THE CONSTRAINT PRODUCED THE FORM. Crossing the feet would have made it dancing, which was forbidden — so the defining rule of the movement is a legal boundary worked around in the body. It is still performed by the McIntosh County Shouters in Georgia, taught rather than reconstructed.

The corn shucking

processiontextual

the plantation South · after the maize harvest · under enslavement · against: nothing

Gatherings to shuck the crop, with a captain, call-and-response songs, and a prize for finding the red ear. Owners recorded it as festivity.

It was unpaid harvest labour with a party attached, and both of those are true at once. The entry keeps both, because dropping either one is how this gets told badly in one of two directions.

Congo Square

noisetextual

New Orleans · Sunday afternoons · under enslavement · against: not applicable

Sunday gatherings with drumming and dance, in effect the only place in the United States where African drumming continued lawfully.

A single permitted square in a single city. The exception proves how complete the prohibition was everywhere else.

Crop Over

processioncontinuous

Barbados · the end of the sugar harvest · under enslavement, and continuous · against: nothing

Marking the end of the cane harvest — the most lethal work in the Atlantic system. It survives as a national festival.

The one harvest rite in this layer that outlived the thing it was held under and kept its name.

The compensation

nonephysical

Britain and Haiti · 1825 and 1837 · abolition · against: not applicable

The Slave Compensation Act 1837 paid roughly £20 million — near 40 per cent of annual Treasury spending — to slave OWNERS for loss of property, financed by borrowing only fully repaid in 2015. Haiti was made to pay France 150 million francs from 1825 for its own independence.

Included in a layer about harvest rites because it is the ledger entry underneath all of them. Restitution is the wrong word for what happened: compensation occurred, and it went the other way.

The church harvest festival

processionphysical

Morwenstow, Cornwall · September · 1843, and continuous · against: nothing

Instituted by the Rev. Robert Hawker in 1843, and now general across British churches and schools.

The one that feels most ancient is the youngest thing here by two thousand years. It is a Victorian invention wearing the clothes of everything above it, and almost nobody singing in one knows that.

what this file refuses

Bronze Age choreography. We have figures with raised arms cut into rock at Tanum and lurs deposited in bogs. We do not have steps, occasions, or reasons. Any entry claiming a Bronze Age mechanism is refused — filling that silence is the commonest failure in this subject and it is always done confidently.
A rite with no mechanism, and a contested claim asserted as settled. 1 entry carries the dispute in a field rather than picking a side quietly in prose — ergot is a real mechanism applied to episodes it may not explain, and this board asserts the fungus without asserting the explanation.
