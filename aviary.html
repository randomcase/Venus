The aviary

the vesper fowl

Vesper because Venus is the evening star, and because a bird is named for the hour it goes up. Every trait below names a real animal that already does the thing, with the figure, because a composite built from things that exist is a design and one built from things that would be nice is a wish. Every trait also says what it costs.

The shell is chalk.

An eggshell is calcium carbonate and the cloud deck is concentrated sulfuric acid. CaCO3 + H2SO4 → CaSO4 + H2O + CO2 is not an edge case — it is the reaction chalk is identified by. A turkey egg carries about 8.5 g of shell, which is undone by roughly 8.3 g of acid. No bird lays outside on Venus. Not with better housing, not with a tougher breed — the shell is the wrong mineral for the sky.
And the dawn has to be manufactured.

Laying is photoperiod-driven: light on a deep-brain photoreceptor starts the cascade that releases the ovum, and a hen lays on a cycle anchored to dawn. The cock calls that boundary and the flock lays against it. Break the boundary and she lays sideways, and no husbandry argues her out of it.

Venus turns once every 116.75 Earth days. So the habitation owes its flock a dawn about 117 times per Venus day while the actual sun manages one, and the dark half of that day runs 58 Earth days on its own. The cock crows at a sun that will not answer for two months. Fighting Apollo again, today — and the light has to come from the habitation, or the eggs stop.

Unless the bird never asked the sun. The zebra finch is an arid-zone opportunist and it does not time laying by daylength at all — it breeds on rainfall, coming into condition within days of water arriving. On a world where the habitation controls the water and cannot control the sun, that is simply the correct cue to have. You do not manufacture 117 dawns for a zebra finch. You turn on a tap.

the traits each one, and the animal that already does it

The body

“turkey”

Domestic turkey, Meleagris gallopavo

A heritage tom runs 8 to 11 kg and a hen 4 to 5. The egg is about 85 g with a shell near 0.42 mm — appreciably thicker than a hen egg’s 0.35 — and a tougher shell membrane under it.

85 g egg, 0.42 mm shell, 8–11 kg tom

what it costsTurkeys eat more per egg than chickens and lay a tenth as often — roughly 100 eggs a year against a leghorn’s 300. You are paying in feed and in time for the mass and the shell.

Strong

“but strong”

Wild turkey, Meleagris gallopavo silvestris

Not an ornamental bird. A wild turkey clears 90 km/h in short bursts, roosts in trees at eight metres, and a tom in display will stand its ground against a dog.

90 km/h burst flight, 8 m roost

what it costsStrength is mass and mass is feed. It also means a bird that can hurt a keeper, and at 50 birds to a person that is not hypothetical.

Learnable

“like the border collie”

Border collie (Chaser) for the standard; African grey parrot (Alex) for the bird

Chaser learned 1,022 proper nouns and could retrieve by name, which is the benchmark the ask names. The avian case is Alex: around 100 labels, plus categories — colour, shape, material — and the use of "none" for an absent quality, which is a concept and not a trick.

1,022 nouns (dog) · ~100 labels + categories (bird)

what it costsTraining does not scale. Chaser took years of daily one-to-one work and Alex thirty years with one researcher. A trait that takes a lifetime per animal collides directly with 50 birds to a keeper, and the board does that arithmetic rather than hoping.

The owl’s senses

“like the owl”

Barn owl, Tyto alba

Ears set at different heights, so the same sound arrives at each at a different time AND a different level — which gives elevation as well as bearing. A barn owl locates prey in total darkness to within one to two degrees. The flight is near-silent: a comb on the leading edge of the primaries breaks the turbulence that makes noise, and velvety down absorbs what is left.

1–2° localisation in full darkness; silent flight

what it costsThe comb costs lift efficiency — silence is bought with drag, which is why owls are slow. And a rod-dense retina that sees in the dark sees colour poorly. You are trading daylight acuity for night work.

It saw a spider three days ago

“it can sit and roost and feed itself just because it saw a spider 3 days ago”

Western scrub jay, Aphelocoma californica; Clark’s nutcracker, Nucifraga columbiana

This is the real one, and it has a name: episodic-like memory. A scrub jay caches perishable food and durable food, and on recovery chooses by HOW LONG AGO it cached — going for the worms when they are still good and the peanuts when they are not. What, where, and when, held together. A Clark’s nutcracker caches on the order of 30,000 seeds across a season and recovers them months later.

what/where/when recall; ~30,000 caches recovered months on

what it costsIt is expensive tissue. The caching corvids carry enlarged hippocampi, and brain is the most metabolically costly gram an animal can grow. A bird that remembers where the spider was eats more to keep the part that remembers.

The rooster

“a rooster will die before it lets a fox”

Domestic cock, Gallus gallus domesticus

It is principle and not calculation. A cock will engage an animal many times its mass and will not disengage; keepers report engagements running for hours. It does not fight to win. It fights so that the thing behind it is not reached.

engages any mass; does not disengage

what it costsIt usually dies. A trait whose expression is the death of the animal expressing it cannot be the plan — it can only be the last line. Any system that relies on it has already failed somewhere earlier.

The hens roost

“hens roost about roosters”

Domestic hen, Gallus gallus domesticus

Roost fidelity is hardwired and it is what makes a flock a flock: at dusk they go up, they settle around the cock, and they stay. It is also why a hen will sit a clutch through disturbance rather than abandon it.

fixed roost at dusk; will not break formation

what it costsTHIS IS THE FAILURE MODE AND IT IS THE MOST IMPORTANT LINE HERE. A fox that gets inside a coop kills on the order of ten birds before the flock breaks, because they will not leave the roost. Surplus killing works precisely because the prey does not disperse. The same instinct that holds them together turns them into a queue.

Fifty to a keeper

“a man can raise possibly 50 or more”

Standard smallholding practice

One person can run 50 to 100 laying birds alongside other work: feed, water, collect, clean, and watch. Above that it stops being husbandry and becomes an operation.

50–100 birds per keeper

what it costsFifty is the ratio for BIRDS, not for trained birds. Individual training is one-to-one and does not divide. Whichever of those two numbers is real, the other one is not, and the board says which.

It is a dinosaur

“it would be like a dinosaur — prehistoric — it’s new every time”

Maniraptora; Gallus gallus is a living theropod

Not a metaphor and not a flourish. Birds are dinosaurs — they are the surviving branch of the theropods, the same clade as Velociraptor, and the wishbone, the hollow bones, the brooding posture over a clutch and the three-fingered hand are all inherited rather than analogous. A cock going up on its toes with its hackles out and driving forward is running a program roughly 150 million years old, and the animal it is running at has no answer prepared. That is what "new every time" means: whatever meets it has met a chicken, and what arrives is not a chicken.

~150 My of theropod defence, unbroken

what it costsAn old program is a fixed one. It cannot be told the odds, it does not learn from the last fox, and it will run at something it cannot beat with exactly the same commitment as at something it can.

It hunts the sun

“a rooster does 2 things: it hunts the sun — the ending the hens lay — and it roosts”

Domestic cock; and the photoperiod physiology of Gallus gallus

The two jobs, and they are the same job. Crowing is anchored to a circadian clock that runs even in constant dim light — a cock kept in the dark still crows near subjective dawn — and the flock takes the day boundary from it. That boundary is not decoration: laying is PHOTOPERIOD-DRIVEN. Light striking a deep-brain photoreceptor sets off the hormone cascade that releases the ovum, and a hen lays on a cycle anchored to dawn. The cock calls the boundary. The hens lay against it. Break the boundary and the laying goes irregular — the hen lays sideways, and there is no husbandry that argues her out of it.

crow anchored to subjective dawn; laying anchored to the crow

what it costsOn Venus there is no dawn to hunt. The solar day is 116.75 Earth days, so outside the habitation the sky does not offer a boundary at anything like the frequency a bird needs. Every dawn has to be manufactured, and the board computes how many.

The red is eaten, not made

“cardinals work well, they are versatile, and you can see a red feather”

Northern cardinal, Cardinalis cardinalis

Versatile is right and it is the reason to keep them: cardinals do not migrate, they hold a territory through a northern winter, and they eat seed, fruit and insects with one heavy conical bill rather than specialising into a corner. But the red is the part worth knowing. Carotenoids cannot be synthesised by any bird — they have to be eaten and then laid into the feather as it grows. So the depth of the red is a record of how well that bird fed during the weeks of the moult. It is not a choice, it cannot be turned up, and it cannot be faked without doing the eating. The cost of forging the signal is exactly the cost of earning it, which is the only kind of mark that never needs an inspector.

carotenoid red; non-migratory; omnivorous bill

what it costsIt is conspicuous to everything, not only to us. A signal legible at distance is legible to whatever else is looking, and the bird pays that whether or not anybody is using it as a marker.

A chief’s son is born grey

“we can mark bounds and make pretty chiefs and pretty chiefs sons”

Northern cardinal, Cardinalis cardinalis — juvenile and post-moult plumage

Follow the pigment and it settles the succession question by itself. A cardinal is not born red. Juveniles are grey-brown with a dark bill, males redden through their first moult, and every adult remakes the colour at every moult afterwards on that year’s feeding. So the mark is real, it is legible at a glance, and it is NOT heritable. A chief’s son inherits the territory, the flock and the name; he does not inherit the red. He eats his way to it or he stays brown in front of everybody.

juveniles grey-brown; red remade at every moult

what it costsA rank that has to be re-earned annually is a rank that can be lost by a bad season rather than by a bad decision. Honest signals are honest about luck too, and a lean year unmakes a chief who did nothing wrong.

Plumage as a settable state

“northern birds”

Rock ptarmigan, Lagopus muta

The counterexample worth having beside the cardinal. A ptarmigan moults white for winter and brown for summer — the mark changes with the season rather than with the bird’s condition. It is a state, not a report. Feathered feet, and it winters above the tree line where nothing else stays.

seasonal white/brown moult; winters above the tree line

what it costsA mark keyed to the calendar and not to the animal tells you the date, which you already knew. It carries no information about the bird carrying it, which is exactly what makes it useless as a chief.

The northern cacher

“northern birds — versatile”

Black-capped chickadee, Poecile atricapillus

The small northern answer to the scrub jay, and it goes further: a chickadee caches thousands of items singly, one seed to a site, and recovers them across a winter. Its hippocampus grows in autumn and shrinks in spring — the memory organ is seasonally rebuilt for the job. It also drops into regulated nocturnal hypothermia, letting body temperature fall by around ten degrees overnight to survive a night it could not otherwise afford.

thousands of single-item caches; hippocampus grows each autumn

what it costsBoth tricks are metabolic loans. Rebuilding brain tissue annually and rewarming from hypothermia every dawn are paid for in food, so the bird that survives the cold night is hungrier at the manufactured dawn than anything else in the flock.

It does not wait for a dawn

“southern birds — hearty”

Zebra finch, Taeniopygia guttata

THIS IS THE ANSWER TO THE DAWN PROBLEM AND IT IS NOT A WORKAROUND. Most birds time laying by daylength, which is why Venus breaks them — the sun there does not offer a boundary at anything like the frequency a bird needs. The zebra finch does not use daylength. It is an arid-zone opportunist: it breeds on RAINFALL, coming into condition within days of water arriving and standing down when it goes. It can raise a clutch on a schedule the weather sets rather than one the sky sets.<br>On a world where the habitation controls the water and cannot control the sun, that is the correct cue to have. You do not manufacture 117 dawns for a zebra finch. You turn on a tap.

breeds on rainfall, not photoperiod; in condition within days

what it costsOpportunism cuts both ways: a bird that breeds whenever conditions allow will also stop whenever they do not, with no seasonal floor under it. And it is a 12 g finch — it answers the timing question and feeds nobody.

Hearty, and loud about it

“now we need southern birds — hearty”

Helmeted guineafowl, Numida meleagris

West African, and the hardiest bird kept by anybody. It takes heat that flattens chickens, forages almost entirely for itself, eats ticks and insects in quantity, and lays an egg with a shell so thick it is difficult to crack by hand — the durability the turkey was asked for, in a smaller package. It is also the best alarm animal in domestic poultry: a flock announces anything new, at volume, for as long as it remains new.

self-foraging; the thickest shell in poultry; continuous alarm

what it costsThe alarm has no volume control and no off switch, and guineafowl are poor mothers — keepers hatch their eggs under hens. A bird that will not raise its own young is not self-sufficient, whatever it does about ticks.

It does not need the water

“southern — hearty”

Muscovy duck, Cairina moschata

South and Central American, domesticated long before Europeans arrived, and the one duck that does not need open water to live well. It perches and roosts — it has claws — it is near-silent where every other duck is not, it eats flies and mosquito larvae in volume, and it broods reliably. On a habitation where every litre is counted, a duck that does not require a pond is a different animal from a duck.

no open water needed; perches and roosts; near-silent

what it costsSlow to mature and strongly dimorphic — drakes run nearly twice the mass of ducks, so the feed goes into birds you keep few of.

What the keeping did to them

“why did we keep birds for so long — they turned red and small and just sang”

Canary, Serinus canaria domestica; Bengalese finch, Lonchura striata domestica; red junglefowl, Gallus gallus

The question is a good one and the record answers it. We kept them for about eight thousand years, and what we selected for is exactly what was named: colour, size, and song.<br>The canary was a streaky green-brown finch from three Atlantic islands. Five hundred years of keeping made it yellow, then red — red canaries only exist because breeders crossed in a South American siskin in the 1920s and then fed carotenoids to hold the colour, which is the cardinal’s trick done deliberately.<br>And the strangest result is the song. The Bengalese finch was domesticated from the white-rumped munia around 250 years ago and its song became MORE complex in captivity, not less — the wild song is constrained by predation and by species recognition, and taking those away let the song run. Keeping them did not simplify them. It removed the thing that had been holding the singing down.

~8,000 yrs (fowl); 500 (canary); song complexity UP in captivity

what it costsDomestication syndrome is the bill: smaller brains relative to body, reduced fear, weaker predator response, and a dependence on the keeper that does not reverse. Every bird on this board is easier to keep and worse at surviving than its ancestor, and those are the same sentence.

A small chicken wing

“so the cardinal is a small chicken wing”

Northern cardinal, Cardinalis cardinalis — as stock rather than as signal

Read plainly: a cardinal is a 45 g bird and the meat on it is a mouthful. Which is the honest reason it is on this board as a MARK and not as a meal — the signal is worth more than the carcass, and that is a real inversion. Songbirds have been eaten historically and the arithmetic has always been bad; the ortolan was a delicacy precisely because it took a great many birds to make a course. A bird kept for what it tells you outlives a bird kept for what it weighs, and it is the only class here where that is true.

~45 g bird; the signal outvalues the carcass

what it costsIt means the flock has a class that eats and never feeds anybody, carried entirely for information. That is a real overhead and it should be argued for on those terms rather than smuggled in.

The rear guard

“we need these birds as a rear guard to civilization”

The flock, collectively

This is the right frame and it is the one the arithmetic already produced. Birds are not the front of anything — they do not clear ground, they do not build, and 366 hens feed a crew of twenty in eggs alone. What they do is HOLD what has already been taken: they occupy a terrain continuously, they notice before people do, they convert waste and insects into food nobody had to ship, and they mark a boundary that stays marked while everyone is looking elsewhere.<br>A rear guard is not the weaker force. It is the force that lets the main body move without losing the ground behind it, and this yard has said before that the mobile rear guard travels alone. So does a cock. So does a barn owl.

holds ground; does not take it

what it costsA rear guard that is mistaken for the main body loses the war. Any plan in which the flock is the food supply rather than beside it has made that mistake, and the keeper arithmetic on this page is what catches it.

It knows the way back

“east to west — these are migratory birds — pigeons”

Rock dove, Columba livia

Three thousand years of carrying messages, and it still works because nobody has fully explained it. A homing pigeon released somewhere it has never been returns to a loft hundreds of kilometres away, and the navigation is layered: a sun compass, a magnetic sense, and — the part that matches the ask almost word for word — an OLFACTORY map. Pigeons with their sense of smell blocked lose the ability to home from unfamiliar ground. They are navigating partly by what the air smells of.<br>It is also the only bird here that is infrastructure rather than livestock. A pigeon is a one-way link that needs no power, no spectrum and no permission, and it is unjammable in the only sense that matters: there is nothing to jam.

homes from hundreds of km; sun, magnetic and olfactory cues

what it costsOne way only. A pigeon flies home and not out, so every link has to be built by carrying the bird the other direction first — the network is only as good as somebody’s willingness to walk.

It leaves before the weather

“things that get up and skip for the slightest smell of rain — snow”

Golden-winged warbler, Vermivora chrysoptera; and waterfowl generally

Documented and stranger than the folklore. In 2014 golden-winged warblers that had just completed migration to Tennessee evacuated again, flying about 1,500 km round trip, one to two days ahead of a tornado outbreak that was still hundreds of kilometres away. The best explanation is INFRASOUND — severe storms emit sound below human hearing that travels enormous distances, and birds hear it.<br>Waterfowl do the coarser version everybody has seen: ducks and geese move on the front itself, dropping with the falling barometer. A bird that gets up and skips at the smell of rain is reading a pressure trace nobody handed it.

evacuated ~1,500 km, 1–2 days ahead of a storm 400 km away

what it costsIt is an unfiltered instrument. The bird acts on the signal and cannot be told the forecast was wrong, so a flock that reads weather will also leave for weather that never arrives.

the roost taxonomy a rooster is whatever holds a roost in that terrain

The roost is the invariant. The bird is what the ground demands — which turns a menagerie into a staffing question, one post per terrain the habitation actually has.

The yard roost

“the rooster”

Domestic cock, Gallus gallus domesticus

A fixed point held by an animal that will not leave it. Fifty hens, one boundary, and a bird whose whole method is presence — it does not patrol, it stands where the thing is.

1 cock : ~50 hens, fixed point

what it costsHolds a point and cannot cover ground. A breach inside is total.

perimeter, dark

The barn roost

“so you get the owl barn — which is a flying rooster”

Barn owl, Tyto alba

The same instinct on a moving perimeter. Silent, nocturnal, and accurate to one or two degrees in complete darkness. Owl boxes in barns predate pest control as an idea.

perimeter patrol; 1–2° localisation, no light

what it costsTerritorial with its own kind, so cover scales by adding ground, never by adding birds.

high ground, open air

The mountain roost

“then you get falcons and eagles which are mountain roosters”

Golden eagle, Aquila chrysaetos; peregrine falcon, Falco peregrinus

High ground held by sight rather than by presence. A golden eagle carries roughly two metres of wing and visual acuity several times human, working prey at kilometres. A peregrine holds a cliff eyrie and arrives from above: the stoop is measured past 380 km/h, the fastest movement of any animal, and it is a roost defended by being higher than everything.

~2 m span; stoop past 380 km/h; sight at kilometres

what it costsNeeds vertical relief and a great deal of air. Put an eagle in a room and you have a large angry bird, not a defence — the whole capability is altitude, and altitude is the one thing a pressurised volume does not have.

water margin

The river roost

“then you get pelicans — river roosters”

Great white pelican, Pelecanus onocrotalus

Water held cooperatively, which is the thing that makes it different from every other entry here. Pelicans fish in a line, swimming abreast to drive fish into the shallows and taking them together. Three metres of wing and a pouch that holds on the order of ten litres. The roost is a shoreline and it is worked as a team.

~3 m span; ~10 L pouch; cooperative line-fishing

what it costsCooperation is the capability, so a single pelican is not a small defence — it is no defence. Below a working number it does not degrade, it stops.

closed cover

The forest roost

“and peacocks — forest roosters”

Indian peafowl, Pavo cristatus

Cover held by sound, because in dense growth sight fails first. Peafowl roost high in trees at dusk and their alarm carries for kilometres through vegetation that stops a line of sight at metres. The train is display and the call is infrastructure; keepers have used peafowl as a warning system for as long as they have kept them, and the reason is the acoustics of a forest, not the tail.

alarm audible at kilometres through closed cover

what it costsThe train is a real cost in flight and in predation, paid for a reason that has nothing to do with guarding, and any bird kept for the alarm is also carrying the tail.

open plain

The plains roost

“you get the emu class — prairie and plains land roosters”

Emu, Dromaius novaehollandiae

Open ground held by mass and distance. Nearly two metres tall, up to 55 kg, sustained running near 50 km/h, and no flight at all — the roost is the horizon and it is held by being able to cross it. In 1932 Australia sent soldiers with machine guns against them and called it off: the birds dispersed, absorbed casualties, and kept the ground. It is the one entry on this list with a war named after it and a win in it.

1.9 m, 55 kg, ~50 km/h sustained, flightless

And the durability answer is here rather than in the turkey. The male incubates for eight weeks WITHOUT EATING, DRINKING OR LEAVING THE NEST, losing about a third of his body mass doing it. That is the most extreme brooding commitment of any bird kept by anybody, and it is the same instinct as the cock in the yard — the roost does not end in my presence — run for two months instead of one night.

what it costsNeeds distance. An animal whose defence is running has nothing at all inside a pressurised volume, and 55 kg of panicking ratite in a corridor is a hull problem, not a guard.

broken ground, shallow soil

The shale roost

“you get the turkey — shale, and shallow, roosters”

Wild turkey, Meleagris gallopavo silvestris

Broken ground, worked shallow. A turkey walks the terrain and feeds by scratching the top few centimetres — litter, seed, insects, whatever is in the first layer — then flies up at dusk to roost eight metres above the ground it just worked. It is the only bird here that holds two elevations in one day, and the reason it can is that it neither patrols like the owl nor stands like the cock. It covers.

forages the top few cm; roosts at ~8 m; 90 km/h burst

Thicker shell and a tougher membrane than a hen egg, at 0.42 mm against 0.35.

what it costsShallow foraging means it is only as good as the top layer, and a deck habitation has no litter layer at all until somebody makes one. This bird needs the soil to already exist.

cold ground, held year-round

The northern roost

“this is a planetary effort, so we need northern birds”

Common raven, Corvus corax; and the resident northern passerines

Cold ground held by staying. The defining northern trait is not toughness, it is NOT LEAVING — raven, cardinal, chickadee and ptarmigan all hold territory through a winter that everything else flies away from. A raven works the same ground at forty below, caches, follows wolves to carrion, and is one of the few animals to pass a mirror-adjacent cognition test. A roost held year-round is worth more than a roost held in summer, because the ground you leave is ground you are negotiating for again next year.

territory held year-round, to −40 °C

what it costsStaying costs calories that migrating does not, so a northern roost is only as good as its winter store — which is why every animal on this line is a cacher, and why the memory trait and the cold trait are the same trait wearing two coats.

hot ground, no season

The southern roost

“now we need southern birds — hearty”

Helmeted guineafowl, Numida meleagris; Muscovy duck, Cairina moschata; red junglefowl, Gallus gallus

Hot ground held by being unfussy. The southern trait is not endurance of cold but tolerance of everything else — heat, parasites, poor forage, and the absence of a season to organise around. The red junglefowl of Southeast Asia is the ancestor of every chicken alive, and it still lives in scrub on whatever it finds. A roost held here is held by an animal that does not require the conditions to be good.

heat- and parasite-tolerant; no seasonal cue required

what it costsNo season means no synchrony. A northern flock lays together and can be worked in one pass; a southern one breeds whenever it likes, so the labour spreads out across the whole year instead of stacking.

the drift, east to west

The drifting roost

“east to west — owls, pigeons, ducks”

Rock dove, Columba livia; migratory owls and waterfowl

The east–west axis is the one Venus changes most, and it changes it in the flock’s favour. At the deck altitude the whole atmosphere superrotates — it laps the planet in about four days while the planet itself takes 243. Anything floating at that height goes with it.<br>Which means every habitation is carried the same direction at the same speed, and RELATIVE TO EACH OTHER THEY ARE STILL. A bird flying between two of them is flying in dead air. The wind that would be 360 km/h against a fixed tower is invisible to a courier between two drifting ones, because it is common to both. The migratory axis on Venus is therefore not the hard one. It is the easy one, and it is easy for a reason nobody would guess from Earth.

the deck laps the planet in ~4 days; relative wind between habitations ≈ 0

what it costsIt is only free while everything floats at the same altitude. A habitation that changes height changes its speed and starts pulling away from the others, and then the courier is flying into the difference — which is a shear, and shear is exactly what a bird cannot cross.

fifty to a keeper and what fifty actually feeds

366 hens
A crew of 20 needs about 1,100 g of protein a day. At 11 g an egg that is 100 eggs a day, and a turkey hen lays around 100 a year — so 366 hens, which at fifty to a keeper is 8 people. That is 40% of the crew keeping birds, for eggs alone.

Eggs do not feed a crew. They supplement one. Any plan that has the flock as the protein rather than beside it has not done this division.
And the fifty is for birds, not trained birds. Chaser took years one-to-one; Alex took thirty. Training does not divide, so whichever of those two numbers is real, the other is not.

the drift east to west, and why it is free

4.4 days
The deck atmosphere superrotates. At about 100 m/s around a circle of 38,370 km, it laps the planet in 4.4 days while the planet itself takes 243.02. Anything floating at that height goes with it.
