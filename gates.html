The gates

a lintel is a beam, and stone cannot bend

Six real lintelled double-leaf gates, drawn, and the one piece of arithmetic that decides all of them. A stone lintel is a beam in bending; stone holds about 4 MPa in tension against forty-plus in compression, so the wall above cracks it and the span at which that happens is small.

And then the arithmetic says something I did not expect. Run it on all six and 6 of 6 sit comfortably INSIDE what their own lintel could carry — and every one of them was given a relieving device anyway. That is the real finding, and it is better than the one I went looking for.

the formula declared, so it can be argued with

σ = M / Z    M = wL²/8    Z = bd²/6    w = ρgbh

ρ = 2600 kg/m³ · σ = 4 MPa · a 1 m lintel under 10 m of wall fails past 4.6 m
That is the entire subject. Everything below is a way of not putting the load on the beam.

The Lion Gate

Mycenae · c. 1250 BCE

span 3.1 mlintel 1 mwall above 6 mleaves 2

relieving triangle

The oldest monumental relieving device standing. Two limestone jambs, a lintel of about twenty tonnes, and above it the courses corbel inward until they meet, leaving a triangular void.

The lions are carved on a slab that FILLS that void. The sculpture everybody photographs is sitting in a hole cut for engineering reasons — the ornament is the structure, and it is the clearest case of it anywhere.

The Treasury of Atreus

Mycenae · c. 1250 BCE

span 2.7 mlintel 1.1 mwall above 13 mleaves 2

relieving triangle

The doorway into the tholos. The inner lintel block is estimated near a hundred and twenty tonnes — among the heaviest single stones set in the Bronze Age.

Even at a hundred and twenty tonnes it still needed the triangle. Mass is not the answer to bending; getting the load off the beam is. They knew that three thousand years ago.

Porta Nigra

Trier · c. 170 CE

span 4.2 mwall above 27 mleaves 2

true arch

A Roman city gate with two arched carriage passages, built dry — the blocks are held by iron cramps and their own weight, with no mortar.

No lintel at all. The Romans stopped arguing with the beam and used an arch for the opening itself, which is entirely in compression and therefore in the one thing stone is good at.

The Meridian Gate

Beijing · 1420

span 5 mlintel 1.2 mwall above 12 mleaves 2

timber lintel

The southern gate of the Forbidden City. Five openings, the central one for the emperor alone, with double leaves studded in nine rows of nine gilt bosses.

It spans five metres on a lintel because the lintel is TIMBER, and timber takes tension. The whole Chinese tradition of wide lintelled openings rests on that one material fact.

The Harlech gatehouse

Gwynedd · 1283

span 3 mwall above 18 mleaves 2

true arch

Twin drum towers holding a passage with two portcullises and double leaves, and murder holes in the vault between them.

The narrowest gate here on purpose. A gate is the one part of a wall that must open, so the answer was to make the opening small and the killing ground behind it long.

The Cour d’Honneur gates

Versailles · 1680s

span 6 mleaves 2

none — nothing is above it

Gilded wrought iron between stone piers, with nothing bearing on it at all.

It carries no load, so it needs no device, and it stopped nobody. In 1789 the crowd came up the road on foot. A gate that holds no weight also holds no one.

Not one of them needed it, and all of them have it. The Lion Gate spans 3.1 m where its own lintel fails past 5.9 m — a factor of nearly two in hand — and they corbelled a triangle over it regardless. I expected the sum to say they had no choice. It says the opposite.
Which is the more interesting answer. They did not build it because a calculation told them to; there was no calculation. They built it because they could not see inside a cyclopean wall, could not predict how irregular rubble would settle onto a beam over three thousand years, and could not afford to be wrong about the one opening in the defences.

And the triangle does not reduce the load on the lintel — it removes the load path completely. That is a different and far stronger guarantee than a safety factor, and it is what you build when you know you cannot compute the thing you are guarding against.
A factor of two is thin. Modern codes sit near 1.5 to 2 on materials you can test, loads you can measure and geometry you drew yourself. Mycenae had none of those. Over-building by that margin is not superstition — it is the correct response to uncertainty you cannot quantify, and it is why the gate is still standing.
Versailles is the control. Six metres of wrought iron with nothing bearing on it, so it needed no device and got none — and it stopped nobody. In 1789 the crowd came up the road on foot. A gate that carries no weight also holds no one.
