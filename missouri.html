Ten sites · the first question

Does the river carry it?

Of the three analyses on offer, this is the only one that can kill the plan. The brick schedule and the plant layout are both questions about how. This one is about whether.

On the numbers. Every hydrological figure here is order-of-magnitude, from published means rather than gauge records, and a real project pulls the USGS record for the specific reach and season. What survives that uncertainty is the shape of the answer — and the shape here is not close enough for the uncertainty to matter.

the physicsOne term does all the work

A free-stream turbine has no dam and no head. It is a rotor in a current, and it takes:

P = ½ · ρ · A · v³ · Cpρ water · A swept area · v velocity · Cp capture, capped by Betz at 16/27 = 0.593

Every term is unremarkable except the cube on velocity. And note what is not in that equation: discharge. How much water the river moves does not appear anywhere. Discharge is the number that impresses in a prospectus and it buys nothing at all per rotor.

Take a third off the velocity and you lose 70 per cent of the power. That single sensitivity decides this whole site list.

the finding

The best site on the list returns 4.2 kW from a three-metre rotor. The estate needs 222 kW on average. That is 53 rotors, at the best site, running continuously, to carry one castle.

And the cube shows itself in the spread. Cape Girardeau at 1.50 m/s against Camdenton at 0.05 — a velocity ratio of 30.0× — is a power ratio of 27,000×. Nothing else in the site list moves numbers like that.

Sites 2 and 9 are not hydro sites. An impoundment has no through-current at the shore, so there is nothing for a rotor to take, and a tailwater below a peaking dam has current only when the dam happens to be generating. Baseload is the one thing they cannot supply, and baseload was the stated reason for choosing them.

The Mississippi sites are the sharpest illustration. Cape Girardeau moves 2.8× the water Hermann does and returns the same power per rotor, because the velocities are the same. It gives you more room for rotors, not more power from one. Discharge is the wrong number to have chosen sites on.

what would workHead, not flow

The equation for a turbine with a drop is a different animal entirely:

P = ρ · g · Q · H · ηQ flow · H head · η efficiency — linear in both, and no cube anywhere

A small Ozark stream carrying 1.2 m³/s down a 6 m drop at 75% returns 53.0 kW from one machine. That is 12.7× what the best free-stream site on this list gives, from a creek you could wade across.

Which inverts the site logic completely. The plan chose the biggest water in the state. The physics wants the steepest, and in Missouri that means the small Ozark streams with a usable fall — not the Missouri, not the Mississippi, and emphatically not a lake.

Everything else in the brief that uses water still works at the big-river sites, and works well: cooling, process water, the fishery loop, the wetland filtration, barge access for finished goods. Those all want volume, and volume is exactly what those sites have. It is only the electricity that was mis-sited, and it was mis-sited because discharge and power got treated as the same idea.

the other numberFive million bricks is off by an order

For scale: Malbork, the largest castle on Earth, took roughly four and a half million bricks — for one. Five million across ten sites is about a tenth of what a single large castle needs. The good news is that site 4 already has the answer in the brief: alluvial clay and an estate brickworks. Do not haul bricks. Make them where the clay is. That is also why Malbork is brick and not stone.

soWhere the analysis should go

  1. Re-site the generation, keep the sites. The ten locations are good for everything they were chosen for except electricity. Pair each with a nearby fall — a tributary, a millrace, an existing low-head structure — and the power problem changes shape entirely.
  2. Cost solar against river. At 222 kW average, a photovoltaic array with storage is the obvious comparison and the brief has not made it. It should be made before any turbine is specified, because if solar wins the river question becomes a process-water question and gets much easier.
  3. Then the brickworks, then the plant layout. Both are real questions and both are downstream of the two above. A construction schedule for a number that is ten times too small would have to be thrown away.

The reason for that order is the same reason this page exists. Answering the how-questions first is how a project spends two years scheduling something that was never going to work.
