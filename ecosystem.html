The Ecosystem

Nine thousand square miles of forest, the size of England, and it is not scenery — it is the machine that makes this planet habitable. Nine orders live in it. Take one out and see what stops working — and what carries on regardless, which is usually the more interesting half.

The nine orders each one has a job, none of them is decoration

Untick one and watch. The capabilities below are not a list of features, they are conjunctions: each one names the orders it actually requires, and it disappears when any of them leaves. Twelve rules for twelve capabilities — adding a tenth animal costs as many rules as it has jobs, not a doubled table.

What the forest can do counted from the panels, not declared

Keep timeneeds fireflies

There is a calendar. Everything else in this wood can be scheduled, compared, replayed and argued about, because there is an agreed answer to when. It cost one signal per flash and no memory of anybody else.

The clock is the cheapest thing in the forest and the only one nothing else can substitute for. See the Pulse for how it settles, and the lessons for how to build one.

Decideneeds owls · fireflies

A quorum can be called, a question can be put, and the answer binds. Note what it requires: not just the owls but the clock, because a vote with no agreed when is nine opinions in a wood rather than a decision.

See the Bench of Nine for what happens when you keep adding judges to fix it, and the Pulse for a chamber that votes on a circuit.

Moveneeds wolves

Something in this wood can act inside a day. It needs no clock and no quorum, which is exactly why it is dangerous and exactly why it is indispensable — an ecosystem of nothing but deliberators does not get slower, it stops.

Proveneeds peacocks · owls

A claim can be made in a form that costs the claimant something, and there is somebody whose job is to read it. Both halves are required. A costly signal with nobody assessing it is just an expensive animal, and an assessor with no costly signal to read is taking everybody's word.

See the flowneeds flamingos · fireflies

The wood has an instrument. You can tell what is moving through it by looking at what the flamingos are made of — and it needs the clock, because a reading with no timestamp is a colour rather than a measurement.

Spreadneeds bees

The forest can reach past where it started. Two trees on opposite sides of a valley are rooted, cannot travel, and will never meet — and the bees make them neighbours anyway. This is the only capability in the wood that is about coverage rather than about function, and terraforming a planet is very largely a coverage problem.

The waggle dance is worth its own line: a bee that has found something returns and dances an angle against the vertical and a duration, and other bees fly to a place they have never seen on the strength of a symbol. That is not signalling that something is good. That is saying where, in an abstraction, to somebody who was not there.

Inheritneeds butterflies

Something in this wood can hold a route that is longer than any life on it. Nobody flying it will see both ends. The generation that arrives is three or four removed from the one that left, they never met, and the way is kept anyway — not written down, not taught, and not dependent on anybody surviving the trip.

The metamorphosis belongs here too, and it is the less sentimental half: a body that dissolves and reassembles as a different animal, with a different food and a different medium, is a system becoming something else without starting again from nothing. Every other order in this wood can only ever be a better version of itself.

Recoverneeds bees · butterflies · fireflies

The forest can come back from a loss. All three are required and none is sufficient: the bees carry the material to where it is needed, the butterflies hold the route across generations that will not overlap, and the fireflies supply the when that makes it a recovery rather than an accident.

Note which orders are not on this list. Coming back does not require anybody to decide it, prove it, report it or move quickly. It requires carriage, memory and a clock, and it happens on a timescale where none of the four louder orders would even register that it had begun.

Pathneeds ants

The wood finds good routes with nobody holding the map. A trail is walked, walking reinforces it, and the reinforcement makes it likelier to be walked again — and no ant on it has ever seen more of the wood than the metre in front of its face.

The half that matters is the evaporation. A trail nobody renews fades on its own, so a route that has stopped being worth walking stops existing without anybody having to notice it, decide about it, or remove it. This is the only structure in the forest that forgets by default, and it is the reason the ground can be used as memory without silting up.

Hold a bearingneeds moths

Something can fly a straight line across a dark wood. The rule is one line: keep a constant angle to a distant light. It works because the moon does not move relative to you as you travel, so the angle you are holding is a direction.

And the same rule, unaltered, puts them in the candle. A light six feet away does change angle as you move, so holding it constant produces a spiral rather than a line. The moth is not malfunctioning. It is a heuristic calibrated to a condition, behaving exactly as specified, in a condition that no longer holds — which is the most common way a working system fails, and the hardest to see from inside it. Every capability on this page has a version of it.

Navigateneeds moths · fireflies · ants

Not a bearing — a fix. Three independent references, and none of them enough alone: a distant light for direction, a clock for elapsed time, and a trail on the ground to correct against. That is what navigation has always actually been, and it is why one reference is a heuristic and three is a position.

Take the ants away and the wood still holds a bearing and still knows the hour, and it has no way to notice it has been flying at a candle for a mile. The third reference is not redundancy. It is the only thing that can disagree with the other two.

Terraformneeds all nine

The full machine. It keeps time, decides, acts, proves what it did, shows what passed through it, spreads past the clearing it began in, and carries the route across lives that do not overlap — which between them is the whole of what making a planet habitable actually consists of — along with the ground to write routes on and a bearing to cross it by, and none of it is the dramatic part.

Nine thousand square miles and nine orders, and the two that matter most to whether any of it is still here in a century are the two smallest animals in the wood: the one that keeps the time and the one that keeps the way.

The clock is goneno fireflies

Look at what is still lit above, because it is not nothing and that is the whole point. The wolves still move. If the peacocks and owls are both here, claims can still be proved. What has stopped is everything that required agreement: nothing can be scheduled, the quorum cannot be called, and the flamingos are a colour rather than a reading.

The bees also carry on, which is worth noticing: the forest keeps spreading while it is unable to decide anything about where. So losing the clock does not stop the forest. It stops the deliberate half and leaves the fast half running — a wood that can still act and can no longer decide, still signal and no longer schedule. That is a worse condition than a wood that has stopped, and it looks considerably healthier from outside.

This page originally claimed that losing the fireflies darkened everything, and enforced it with a blanket rule at the bottom of the stylesheet. The rule lost, silently, to every capability that named two orders — because those selectors carry more specificity and cascade order only settles ties. The comment at the foot of the stylesheet keeps the arithmetic. The claim was wrong before the rule was; fixing the rule would only have made a false statement enforceable.

Bare rock nothing present

No orders at all. This is what the planet was before the forest, and it is the state the page opens into if you clear every box — designed for deliberately rather than fallen into, because a system with nothing sensible to say about being empty is not finished.

The capability count counts itself. Each panel carries a marker that increments a CSS counter — and a marker inside a hidden panel increments nothing, because a box that was never generated cannot carry a counter. So that number is the number of panels actually showing. It is not a figure anybody typed, and it cannot drift away from what is on the screen, which is the only reason it is allowed to be printed at all.

Why these nine and not nine nicer ones

Every animal here does a real job that the others cannot, and each job is a real mechanism rather than a mood. Fireflies genuinely synchronise by pulse coupling and it genuinely needs no leader. Owls are a committee and committees genuinely fail by abstention. A peacock's train is the standard example of costly signalling, and the reason it works is that it is wasteful — the waste is load-bearing. A flamingo really is pink because of what it eats, which makes it the rarest thing in any system: an instrument that cannot be configured to say something convenient.

Wolves are two. They were always two. They are not here to be defeated; they are here because a wood with no velocity in it is a wood that deliberates its way to a stop, and because an audit that has never been tested against anybody quick is not an audit, it is a habit.

The bees and the butterflies were added last and they are the two the wood would miss longest. Everything the other five do, they do now: this decision, this claim, this reading, this hour. Carriage and inheritance are the only two jobs in the forest whose whole value is measured after the animal doing them is gone — a tree pollinated by a bee that died in the summer, a route flown by four generations of an insect that never met each other. A wood with the five and not the last two works perfectly and ends with the animals currently in it.

The ants and the moths are the two that are about being wrong. An ant trail is a claim about the world that decays unless the world keeps agreeing with it, which is the only self-correcting memory in the wood. A moth is a rule that is right for a thousand generations and then meets a candle, which is the only honest picture anywhere on this page of how a good system actually fails: not by breaking, but by continuing to work perfectly in a condition it was never calibrated for.
