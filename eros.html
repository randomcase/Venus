the parable · one

All things, and never loved

A story about the difference between being looked at and being loved — and about what a look does to the one doing it.

the myth Psyche was a mortal princess, the youngest of three, and so beautiful that travellers began leaving at her feet the offerings that belonged to Aphrodite. Crowds came. Her sisters, plainer, were married. She was admired by everyone and asked for by no one — the text is explicit that men worshipped her as a goddess and therefore did not court her as a woman.
the reading That is the whole problem stated in the first paragraph, before any god does anything. Being looked at is not a lesser form of being loved. It is a different thing that occupies the same space and keeps love out.

the parable · two

When he is not looking, she is

The switch is set to look away. In that condition she holds the goddess's place — and this is not a metaphor the page is adding. In the story the worship physically moves: altars go cold at Cythera and Paphos, and the offerings are carried to a mortal girl's doorstep instead.

You have looked. The name has changed and so has everything under it — she is not the goddess any more, she is the girl nobody married.

the myth Aphrodite, finding her temples deserted, sends her son to make Psyche fall in love with the most wretched thing alive. He goes with the arrow ready. He sees her. He does not fire.
the reading Note what he is at that moment: he has come to look at her, in order to do something to her. And the look does not land the way it was sent. The story's hinge is a gaze that fails to stay a gaze.

the parable · three

And when he looks because she is beautiful, he is

Set the switch to look at her and watch both names change at once. That simultaneity is the claim this page is making.

There it is. He is Himeros now — desire, the impetuous one, Eros's brother among the Erotes and not the same god. And she is Psyche, unloved: back at the start, worshipped and unmarried. One switch moved both.

the myth The Erotes are several. Eros is love; Himeros is desire that cannot wait; Pothos is longing for what is absent. Ancient art puts them together and tells them apart — they are not synonyms, and a poet choosing between them is saying something specific.
the reading So the transformation is available in the vocabulary itself. If what moves him is that she is beautiful, the tradition already has a name for that and it is not Eros. And under a desiring look she cannot be beloved, only wanted — which returns her exactly to panel one. All things, and never loved.

the parable · four

The condition was the dark

the myth He takes her to a house she cannot see the owner of. He comes only at night. The single rule is that she must never try to see his face — not that she must not touch him, or ask, or leave. Only that she must not look.

Her sisters visit and do the work sisters do in this kind of story: they tell her she is sleeping with a serpent, and that a woman who cannot see her husband has no husband. So she is given a lamp and a knife and told to look while he sleeps.

the reading Read the prohibition as cruelty and it is arbitrary. Read it as a condition and it is precise: darkness is the only arrangement in which the look cannot happen, and therefore the only one in which he stays Eros and she stays beloved rather than admired. He is not hiding a monstrous face. He is holding off a transformation.

the parable · five

She lights the lamp

the myth She raises the lamp and sees not a serpent but the god himself, asleep and beautiful. She is so taken by the sight that her hand shakes — and a drop of hot oil falls on his shoulder. He wakes, and he goes. The knife is never used. What ends it is the looking.

The switch is still on look away, so the lamp above is burning and the room is not empty yet.

You have thrown it, so the flame is out and there is only smoke. That is not decoration — in the story the light is what puts the light out.

the reading The drop of oil is the detail that gives the game away. She is burned into losing him by the very thing she used to see him with, and the wound is on him. A look given because someone is beautiful does damage in both directions: it costs her the beloved and it costs him being loved.

the parable · six

It does not end in the dark

the myth She searches. Aphrodite sets her four tasks: sort a mountain of mixed grain; take wool from the murderous golden rams; fill a jar from the falls of the Styx; and go down to Persephone for a box she is forbidden to open. She opens it, of course, and falls into a sleep like death. He finds her, wakes her, and takes the case to Zeus — who makes her immortal. They marry as equals. Their daughter is named Pleasure.
the reading So the parable is not that love requires blindness. The dark was a holding action, and it failed, as holding actions do. What actually resolves it is that she stops being the looked-at: she is made a god, and a god can be looked at without being diminished by it. The problem was never the eyes. It was the asymmetry. Two mortals cannot fix that with a rule about lamps; it took an apotheosis.

The myth is Apuleius, Metamorphoses, book four to six — a tale told to a kidnapped girl by an old woman in a cave, which is its own comment on who these stories get told to. Everything in the amber panels is the text. Everything in the gold ones is a reading, and it came out of a conversation rather than a commentary.
