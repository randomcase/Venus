Twelve Grades

One curriculum per level, preschool to grade ten, in a single strand: computational thinking, objects first and a browser later. No level claims a duration; every level has a test, and every level is walked twice — by the cadet who did the whole syllabus and by the one who commissioned himself at level 4. no script †

What this is, exactly said before anything else

Twelve curricula in one strand. There is no reading here, no writing, no science, no history, no arts, no physical education, and no number sense beyond what this strand needs. A page calling itself the grade three curriculum while covering a single strand would be doing the exact thing the rest of this yard spends forty pages refusing to do, which is print a figure it did not compute. This is a strand. It sits inside a curriculum; it is not one.

The first six levels have no computer in them. That is not a position about screens. It is that a group, counting on, a label that means something, a rule that does not change, a table and an order that matters are all easy to learn with objects on a table and nearly impossible to learn for the first time inside a text editor. By level 6 there is a file. By level 11 there is a working board.

Two cadets the same twelve levels, two postures

Every level below carries a note on how two very different students meet it, because the twelve levels are not the interesting part — the posture you bring to them is. Bumblebee is not a scout and never was a drone: he is the cadet who did the entire syllabus, in order, including the six levels with no computer in them, and who has never once claimed a rank. Starscream had no patron and no sponsor and was never going to be given anything, so he commissioned himself — sat exams nobody set, took the lieutenancy at level 4 and dared the academy to take it back.

The academy did not take it back, because he was not lying: he could do levels 0 to 4 better than the cadets who were given them. What he could not do was 5 to 8, and he had to come back for those in public, years later, at a cost the levels themselves never carried. Both of them finish. Neither route is free, and the page is not going to pretend one of them is the moral of the other. The academy grades the board, not the badge.

A homage, not a licence: these are two well-known characters used as study postures because everybody already knows the shape of them. Nothing here is quoted from anywhere and nothing here is affiliated with anyone.

There is no fragment in the address, so this page does not know which level you want. That is the right state for a first visit and it is selected for on purpose rather than left as whatever happens.

Place by test, not by age. Every level below states one thing a learner can either do or cannot do. Start two levels below where you think they are and move up as fast as the tests allow — that costs an afternoon and it is the cheapest mistake available here. A child who passes level 4's test at six should be given level 5. A fourteen-year-old who cannot pass level 2's should be given level 2, without commentary, and will usually clear three levels in a week.

No level says how long it takes. Those numbers are invented wherever they appear, and in this strand more than most: the ages at which people arrive at a rule that does not change vary by years and the variation predicts nothing about where they finish.

preschoolGroupsno computer

The whole level is one idea: a group is a thing you can point at. Buttons that are red. Cups that are big. The group is real even though you cannot pick it up, and it stays the same group when you move the pieces around. Everything in the eleven levels after this is built on being able to hold a collection in mind as one thing.

Make

A sorting tray. A pile of mixed objects and two or three empty trays, and no instruction about what the categories are — let them choose. Then ask for a different sort of the same objects.

One-to-one alongside it: one cup, one saucer, one spoon. Set the table for the number of people actually in the room.

Say out loud

“These are together because…” — and let the reason be anything, including a wrong one. The reason being sayable matters more than the reason being right, and correcting it here costs more than it buys.

At the academy

BumblebeeSorts the same crate three ways across three days and then asks for a fourth tray, because he has thought of a category the trays do not cover yet.
StarscreamSorts it once, correctly, faster than anyone, and announces that the categories are now official. They are good categories. Nobody agreed to them.

Naming a group is not the same as owning it, and this is the level where the difference is cheapest to learn.

Test

They sort the same objects two different ways on two different days and can say what the difference is, without counting anything.

kindergartenCounting onno computer

Adding is counting on from a number you already have, not starting again at one. That single move is what a counter does, it is what every board in this yard does, and a learner who restarts at one every time will hit a wall in level 8 that looks like a CSS problem and is not.

Make

A tally chart of something real and ongoing: birds at a window, red cars, people who came to the door. One mark per event, made at the time, over several days.

Then: three more arrive. Do not recount. Say the number you had and count on from it.

Why the tally

Because the marks stay. The chart is a record that outlives the counting, and a child who has kept one has met the idea that state can be written down and picked up again later — which is level 6 of the yard school, twelve years early.

At the academy

BumblebeeKeeps the tally for eleven days, including the four days when nothing happened at all, because a zero is a reading.
StarscreamRecounts the whole pile from one every single time and is still first with the answer, which is why nobody notices for a year that he never learned to count on.

Speed hides a missing idea better than slowness hides a present one.

Test

Given a pile they have already counted and three more objects, they say the new total without starting from one.

grade 1Labelsno computer

Write the number on the thing. A label is a promise: this object is worth this much, every time, and if the total is wrong you can find out why by reading the labels instead of arguing about it. This is the whole design of every board in this yard, stated for six-year-olds, and it is not a simplification of it.

Make

A shop. Real objects, a written price on each one, paper money. Buy three things and add them. Then have an adult quietly change one price tag between rounds and let them find it.

The move that matters

When the total is wrong, nobody guesses. You go back and read the tags. A learner who does that once has learned the habit the whole strand is for, and the alternative habit — deciding what the answer probably is — is very hard to remove later.

At the academy

BumblebeeWrites the number on every part before anybody asks him to, and again after he changes one.
StarscreamRemembers all the prices. This works, genuinely works, right up until somebody else changes one and there is nothing to check against.

The label is not for you. It is for the person who was not in the room.

Test

You change a price without telling them, the total comes out wrong, and they find the changed tag by checking rather than by remembering.

grade 2Rules that do not changeno computer

Same in, same out. A rule is a promise about every future case, not a description of the last one. The point of the level is that a rule can be tested by prediction: say what will come out before you put it in, and you have learned something either way.

Make

A machine out of a shoebox. In one side, out the other, and one written rule taped to the lid — add two, swap the colour, double it. Someone else operates it; the learner predicts.

Then the hard version: they operate a secret rule and someone else has to work out what it is by feeding it things.

The failure to look for

A rule that works on four examples and not the fifth. Do not rescue it — write the fifth case down next to the rule and let the rule be visibly wrong. That is a better lesson than a rule that survives because nobody tested it hard.

At the academy

BumblebeeTests his rule on a fifth case, finds it wrong, and writes the fifth case down next to the rule instead of quietly dropping it.
StarscreamHas a rule that has never failed. It has been tried four times, all of them by him, all of them on examples he chose.

A rule that has only ever been confirmed is a description wearing a uniform.

Test

They predict the output before it comes out, and when they are wrong they change the rule rather than the example.

grade 3Tables · two things at onceno computer

Some answers need two keys. Not hot and not Tuesday, but hot on a Tuesday. A grid with rows and columns holds an answer in every cell, and the answer is not derivable from either edge alone. This is compound :has(), seven levels early, and children of eight are entirely comfortable with it.

Make

A real decision table for something that actually happens: weather down the side, day of the week across the top, what we do in each cell. Fill every cell. Leave none blank because it “never happens” — that cell is where the argument will be.

The cost, introduced honestly

Add one more kind of weather and count the new cells. Four columns by three rows was twelve; five by three is fifteen. Nobody computed that for you and nobody will. This is the exact bargain the yard's lookup tables make and it is worth meeting when the numbers are small.

At the academy

BumblebeeFills every cell, including the three that describe things which have never happened and probably will not.
StarscreamLeaves the impossible cell blank, because it is impossible. That cell is where the argument starts, eight years later, in front of everybody.

The blank cell is not empty. It is a decision nobody has to admit to having made.

Test

Given a table with one cell blanked out, they fill it and can say why that answer and not the one from the row next door.

grade 4Orderno computer

The last instruction wins, and some steps cannot move. Sequence is not decoration: swap two steps in a recipe and you get a different object or no object. The skill is finding which pairs can be swapped freely and which one step has to be last — because in level 10 an entire enforcement mechanism will rest on exactly that and nothing will warn you.

Make

Written instructions for something physical with a real failure mode — a sandwich, a paper aeroplane, feeding an animal. Hand them to somebody who follows them exactly and unhelpfully.

Then: deliberately swap two steps and watch it fail. Then find two steps that can be swapped with no effect, which is harder and more useful.

The one to name

“Wash your hands” goes first. “Close the gate” goes last. Ask which step is the closing-the-gate step, every time, for the rest of the strand.

At the academy

BumblebeeFinds the step that has to be last and can say why the two before it could trade places.
StarscreamReorders the entire procedure so that the step which has to be last is his. This is not cheating and it is the level where he is simply better.

Understanding that order is authority is a real skill. Being the last step is a different one, and this is the only level where the second is worth more.

Test

Given a list of instructions, they can point at the step that must be last and at two that could trade places, and be right about both.

grade 5A page is a filethe computer arrives

HTML, and only the parts that carry meaning: headings, paragraphs, lists, links, images. The page is a file on a disk that you can open, change and reload. Not an app, not an account, not a service — a file. A learner who understands that owns their work permanently, and one who does not will spend years renting it.

Make

One page about something they genuinely care about, written in a plain text editor, opened from the disk by double-clicking it. Then edit, save, reload. Then a second page and a link between the two.

Copy template.html if you want a working starting point, but a blank file is better here.

Do not skip

Show the file in the folder. Rename it. Move it. Email it to themselves and open it on a different machine. The point of the level is not the tags, it is that the thing exists as an object.

At the academy

BumblebeeKeeps his page on a disk he owns, opens it by double-clicking it, and can hand the whole thing to anybody on a stick.
StarscreamBuilds inside somebody else's system because it was faster and better, which it was, and then cannot take any of it with him when he leaves.

The fastest way to build the thing and the way to still have it are not always the same way, and level 6 is where that bill is small.

Test

Their page survives being moved to another computer and opened there, and they can say why the picture broke and how to fix it.

grade 6The cascadestyle, and who wins

CSS: selectors, properties, and the rule that decides fights — later wins over equally specific. Level 4 was this idea with a sandwich. This is the same idea with a stylesheet, and it is the first time the learner will meet a failure that produces no error message at all: the rule is fine, it is simply losing.

Make

Take the grade 5 page and theme it twice from one stylesheet, then a third time by changing five colour variables at the top and nothing else. Then write two rules that fight, on purpose, and predict the winner before reloading.

Read

bare.html — 105 lines, and by the end of this level they will recognise perhaps half of it. That is the right amount and it should be said, or they will think they have failed.

At the academy

BumblebeeWrites a comment above every rule that is load-bearing on its position, so the next cadet through is not ambushed by it.
StarscreamUnderstands later-wins immediately and without being taught, because he has spent his entire career arranging to be standing later than everybody else.

He is right about the cascade before anybody explains it. He is also the reason the comment is needed.

Test

Shown two rules that both apply, they say which one wins and why, before opening the browser.

grade 7State without programming:checked and :has()

A checkbox is a variable the browser stores for you. :checked reads it, :has() lets an ancestor be styled by what is inside it, and between them you have a state machine with nothing to keep in sync. No script, and it is not a trick — this is what those selectors are for.

Make

A page that shows different content depending on what is ticked. Then gate one control behind another: a second set of options that does not exist until the first is answered.

Then scope a rule wrongly on purpose and watch precisely nothing happen. Sit with the silence. That is the failure mode for the rest of their life in this material.

Read

cruise-control.html and learn.html, mistake one — before they make it, not after.

At the academy

BumblebeeScopes to the right ancestor, because he read mistake one before making it rather than after.
StarscreamScopes to the row, gets silence, blames the browser for two days, and writes a very persuasive memo about a bug that does not exist.

Nothing errors. That is the whole hazard of this level and no amount of talent substitutes for having been warned.

Test

When one of their rules does not fire, the first thing they check is which ancestor it is scoped to, and they are usually right.

grade 8Counting, in the pagecounters

CSS counters: declare on body, increment on the label beside a checked input, print with content:counter(). Level 1 was counting on with a pencil; this is counting on with a browser. The browser adds. It does not subtract, multiply, divide or take a minimum — and that sentence is the seed of the next two levels.

Make

A board with six checkboxes and three totals, every number written on the label where it is selected. No hidden model. Then remove one number from a label and find where the total goes wrong — which is the grade 1 shop again, and say so.

The constraint, at last

A counter prints only after the things that increment it. Inputs first in the source, totals after, layout put back with grid. Move the totals up and watch them go to zero. Every page in this yard is built around that one rule.

At the academy

BumblebeePrints only what the page computed, and where it could not compute something he prints the parts and says why the difference is absent.
StarscreamPrints the number he expects. He is right about it. That is worse than being wrong about it, because nothing on the board will ever tell him the day he stops being right.

A correct figure the page did not compute is a fault that has not surfaced yet.

Test

Point at any number on their board and they point at the line of markup that produced it. No exceptions, including the ones they forgot about.

grade 9Two keys, and the price of themcompound :has()

:has(A):has(B) — grade 3's decision table, written as rules. There is no multiplication in this language, so you write the products: three options by two states is six rules, and adding a fourth option costs two more. The table is visible and someone typed every cell, which is a better property than most computed tables have.

Make

A 3 × 2 lookup board with the table printed on the page beside the rules, so the two can be checked against each other. Then add an axis and pay the cost without complaining about it.

Read

pulse.html — two seats read two keys each and both tables are printed. bench.html — an assignment rule three keys deep, with every reachable cell written out by hand and the counters there to audit the hand.

At the academy

BumblebeePays for the fourth axis in rules, writes all eight, and prints the table beside them so the two can be checked against each other.
StarscreamThis is the level he skipped when he commissioned himself. He comes back for it in public, years later, and it costs him more than sitting it in order ever would have.

Self-commissioning works exactly as far as the levels you actually did. The gap does not close on its own and it does not stay hidden.

Test

They can state what a new axis costs in rules before adding it, and they no longer treat that number as a problem to be got around.

grade 10The whole board, and the ethicthe last level

Transport — <form method="get"> hands state to the next page. Persistence — the URL fragment and :target are the only durable state a scriptless page has. And then the part with no API behind it: no number on a page may be one the page did not compute. If you cannot compute it, print the parts and say why the difference is absent. Where a comparison is made by hand, enumerate it and print the counters that let a reader check your hand.

Make

A complete board of their own about something real, with a URL state, a document it hands on, and a stop rule at the bottom of the stylesheet that they can explain. Then find the one number on it the page did not actually compute — there is always one — and either compute it or replace it with the parts.

Then

The yard school, units 5 to 7, which is this level again at adult pace and with the arguments included. persist.html for the store. chamber.html for what it looks like when a board is honest about what it is not measuring.

At the academy

BumblebeeBuilds a board and writes on it what it does not measure. He has never claimed a rank. He is given one, which he finds mildly embarrassing.
StarscreamBuilds a better-looking board that does not say what it is not measuring. The lieutenancy he took at level 4 is by now entirely real, earned out of order, at three times the price.

Both of them finish. The academy grades the board, not the badge — and the badge was never the thing either of them actually wanted.

Test

Somebody asks where a figure on their board came from and the answer is never “it looked about right”. That is the end of the strand and it is the only part of it that transfers to everything else.

Half of this strand has no computer in it and the counter says so on purpose. Six of the twelve levels are objects on a table, and if that column reads low while the total reads high, somebody has been started in the middle — which is survivable and worth knowing. These ticks do not survive a reload: which level you are reading is one value and it lives in the URL, but twelve independent ticks would need an anchor for every combination, and there are four thousand and ninety-six of those. So they are checkboxes, they are transient, and the page says so instead of implying a durability it does not have.

All twelve cleared

One selector with twelve :has() in it, written last in the stylesheet, showed this panel — grade 9 and grade 4 shaking hands on the way out. A learner who has cleared all twelve can build any page in this yard and, more usefully, can say what each one is not measuring.

Where they go next is not another level. It is the yard school at adult pace, or a real problem belonging to somebody else, which is the only assessment in this strand that was ever going to count.
