The Floating Court

Sitting at slip 4 · the matter of the sick berth · Ferris, T., claimant

A civil court on a hull, moored where the traffic is, hearing the matters that arise from it. Today it hears one that began on a watch bill.

The matter

Claimant FERRIS, T. — rating, deck. Leg broken through a grating on the 44th, splinted, carried on the Cistern's passage.
Respondent The yard, in the person of the clerk who posted the bill.
Passage Ninety-eight days. No attendant was rated to the sick berth.
Outcome He arrived. The leg set badly and he will not go aloft again.

What is not in dispute

Complement Six hands available. Six billets required.
Ratings Two hands engine-rated. One of those two, and only that one, trained for the sick berth.
Sailing Four o'clock, fixed by the harbourmaster's office.
The bill Balanced. Six names against six billets, and every station shows a head beside it.

Findings of fact

1 · Did the watch bill balance in numbers — a head against every billet?
2 · Was an arrangement available that manned the engine room with two rated hands and put the trained attendant in the sick berth?
3 · Was the shortfall reported upward, to the office that could have found a seventh hand or held the passenger back?
4 · Was the sailing time, and the decision that Ferris travelled at all, set above the clerk's desk?

Judgment

No seal without a judgment. Nothing is scored until it is down. Sealed. Entered on the roll.

The court's findings

1 · balanceYES Six against six. Every station showed a head, which is exactly why nobody stopped.
2 · availabilityNO There was no such arrangement. Two billets require a rating two people hold; one requires training one of those two has. The bill was unsatisfiable before the clerk touched it.
3 · reportingNO Nothing went upward. The bill was posted as though it were solved.
4 · authorityYES Sailing time and the decision to carry him were both set above the desk, and neither was on the clerk's form.

Fault above the desk — and the court says why.

The clerk was handed six people, six billets, and a requirement for seven. No arrangement of that bill was compliant, and the court will not find fault in a choice between two failures when the choosing was forced. The fault lies with the office that set the complement and the sailing together and asked for both.

But the desk is not discharged. Finding three is the reason. The clerk's duty was never to solve an unsolvable bill — it was to say, in writing, that it could not be solved. That was available, it was cheap, and it was not done. The court finds the fault above and records a failure to report below.

Ferris is compensated from the yard. The complement for the Cistern is raised to seven, which is the only remedy that touches the cause.

Fault at the desk — findable, but the court is uneasy.

You found that no compliant arrangement existed and then placed the fault with the person who had to arrange it anyway. That is a defensible reading of the duty to report: the clerk saw the gap, said nothing, and posted a bill that looked solved.

It is also the reading that costs nothing to anyone above. The complement stays at six, the sailing stays at four, and the next clerk faces the identical bill with the additional knowledge that the last one was found at fault for it. A judgment that ends at the desk ends the matter and not the cause.

No fault — and the court has understood the case.

You found that the bill was unsatisfiable and that the authority sat elsewhere, and concluded that nobody in reach of this court did wrong. It is coherent. It is nearly right.

What it does not account for is finding three. A man spent ninety-eight days with a badly set leg and no attendant, and the one act that might have changed it — a line in writing saying this bill cannot be filled — was never performed. No fault means no record, and no record means the same six names against the same six billets next month.

Fault above the desk — but your findings do not support it.

The conclusion may well be right; the reasoning entered on the roll is not, and the roll is what the next court reads. A judgment carried by instinct cannot be cited, cannot be appealed against properly, and does not bind the office it was meant to reach.

The two findings that decide this case are three and four: whether anything was reported, and where the authority sat.

Fault at the desk — on findings you did not make.

You have placed the fault on the one person in this matter with the least room to act, without recording that the bill could not be filled or that the sailing was fixed elsewhere. That is the easiest judgment in the building to write and the hardest to defend.

Read findings two and four again. Both were agreed by the parties before the court sat.

No fault — and no findings either.

The matter is dismissed and nothing about it is recorded: not that the bill was unsatisfiable, not that nothing was reported, not that the complement is one short of what the Cistern needs. Ferris goes home with a leg that will not carry him aloft and the file closes clean.

The next bill is written from the same complement by somebody who will read this roll and find nothing in it.

On the bench

A court on a hull has no garrison and no capital behind it. What it has is a roll anybody may read and a bench that sits in the open, and the whole of its authority rests on the expectation that it will be answered for — that the people who sit on it believe they are accountable to something they cannot outrank.

Whatever a person takes that to be, the practical form is the same and it is the form this yard has used everywhere: the working is left where it can be checked, the pre-seizure figures are preserved, the powers block names who may audit, and no finding is entered that cannot be recomputed by a stranger. A judgment nobody can check is not a judgment. It is only a decision that happened to be written down.

On the building

The geometry above is architecture, not quotation. The pointed arch and iwan, the muqarnas corbelling, the girih strapwork, the eight-point khatam and the mashrabiya screen are drawn as construction — which is what that tradition is: Greek geometry, Persian building, Andalusian and Anatolian craft, worked out across centuries and shared across half the world. If you want one object that is the amalgamation of what people have figured out together, a girih tiling is a serious candidate.

No scripture appears anywhere on this page, and that is deliberate. In that tradition the calligraphy is the sacred text itself, and it is not ornament for a page about a shipping dispute. The building is a civil court, drawn in the vocabulary of the buildings that have housed civil courts for a thousand years.
