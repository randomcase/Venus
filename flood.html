The Flood Pulse

Ten floods a year beat one lake, and the reason is ecological rather than hydraulic. Then the gravity ledger for the people digging it — which has a line in it that does not move. no script †

1 · The pulse ten arrivals · and the edge is where the work happens

Junk, Bayley and Sparks named it in 1989 and it is still the governing idea in floodplain ecology: the productivity of a river-floodplain system is driven by the predictable pulse, not by the channel and not by standing water. The advancing and retreating edge — the moving littoral, the dashed band above — is where nutrients are released, where decomposition runs, and where nearly all the production is. Ten arrivals sweep that edge across the floodplain twenty times a year. A lake sweeps it never.

One permanent lake

Standing water, a fixed shoreline, and a bottom that goes anoxic. Production concentrates in a narrow ring and stays there. It is also the version that needs 29.3% of all the water on Venus, so it was never available anyway.

Ten pulses down a tributary

Nutrients delivered onto the plain and organic matter returned to the channel, twenty times a year. Needs a river — 4.5×1010 kg, about one thousandth of one per cent of the planet’s water, and three years of condensing at a hundred dive units.

Which answers the ocean question from the other side. You could not have built a sea, and you would not have wanted one. The thing that grows a forest is not water sitting on it — it is water arriving and leaving, on a schedule the plants can predict.

2 · The gravity ledger 8.71 m/s² at the deck · 88.8% of home

The surface figure is 8.87, but the deck is 55 km up and altitude costs another 1.6%, so the number the work actually happens in is 8.71 m/s². Pick a task and see whether that helps — because for half of them it does not, and the half that does not is the half that hurts people.

Mass did not change. Only weight did. A full barrow is easier to hold up and exactly as hard to get rolling, exactly as hard to stop, and a load swinging on a rope has precisely the momentum it always had. A crew that spends a fortnight finding everything lighter will misjudge the first thing that has to be stopped rather than held, and that is the accident this table exists to prevent.

3 · The dig 4.5×107 m³, by hand

A channel 300 km long, 50 m wide and 3 m deep is 45 million cubic metres of excavation. Hand digging runs about a cubic metre per person-day on Earth; here the carrying gain lifts that to 1.13, and no further — because the swing is muscle and the ground resists the same.

Those are honest numbers and they are the argument for the dust bowl doing some of the work. A convection cell over a hot floor sorts and moves material for nothing, continuously, for as long as the hole exists — and a channel that the wind is already helping to scour is a channel fewer people have to dig. The excavation and the circulation are the same project.
