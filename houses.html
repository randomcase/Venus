the claim, and its limits

This list calls itself exhaustive over the luxury fashion, lingerie and accessory houses, by owner, as of 2025. That word is only worth printing beside the things it leaves out, so here they are.
  1. Mass-market and fast fashion, except where a house set the terms of a category it no longer leads (Victoria's Secret, Calvin Klein) and leaving it out would misrepresent the sector.
  2. Sportswear and streetwear, except where a luxury group owns it outright.
  3. Beauty and fragrance as standalone businesses; included only where a fashion group holds them.
  4. Fine jewellery below the maison level, and every independent bench jeweller.
  5. Regional houses in markets this list still under-covers: the Gulf and the Levant, Central and South America outside Colombia and Brazil, Southeast Asia. Africa was on this line until a cohort of 25 houses closed it — which is what an exclusion is for: a debt the list admits to, not a wall it hides behind.
  6. Bespoke workshops with no brand — the majority of Naples tailoring, most Northampton lasting, every atelier working under someone else's label.
  7. Houses that closed before 2000, and revivals that never resumed production.
Ownership moves faster than any list. Prada took Versace from Capri in 2025; Richemont sold YNAP the same year; Off-White's trademark left LVMH for Bluestar in 2024. Every ownership field here is a snapshot.

Founding years for the smaller and newer houses are the least reliable field, which is why the layer allows a null year and the board counts them out loud rather than filling them in.

'Luxury' has no agreed boundary. This list draws it at houses whose price, distribution or making is deliberately restricted, which admits some accessible houses and excludes some expensive ones.

The African cohort is a survey and a thin one. 25 houses across a continent is a beginning, not coverage, and the countries represented are the ones with the loudest fashion weeks rather than the ones with the most making.
How to break it. Name a house that meets the asserted scope, is not in the list, and is not covered by an exclusion above. One such house falsifies the claim, and the honest response is to add it rather than to widen an exclusion until it fits.

Ready-to-wear and lingerie sit at the bottom because theirs barely does — which makes them the closest thing luxury has to a fungible good, and it is no accident that they are also the hardest categories to hold a price in.

the four groups

Mind the denominator. Nine of these cohorts are a group’s actual roster — nobody chose who is inside LVMH. The other nine are surveys, and in those I deliberately over-included independents: the Naples tailors, the Northampton lasters, 140 lingerie houses. A percentage taken over all 544 would measure that choice and report it as a fact about the industry. So: of the 147 houses held in a group here, 85 (58%) are in LVMH, Kering, Richemont or Prada — and even that is a share of the houses named here, not of the market.
It is a concentration either way, and not a proof. Lesson two’s test asks whether removing one party disconnects the rest, and no single group here holds a position that does that — which is the definition of a syndicate rather than a monopoly, and the reason single-deletion tests keep coming back clean on this market.
When Capri and Tapestry tried to merge in 2023 a court blocked it in 2024, on the reasoning that the two together would have held the accessible-handbag market between them. That is a regulator running the cut-set test under another name, and reaching the answer the arithmetic gives.

on the lingerie cohorts 140 houses, 55 of them men’s

France is the centre of gravity and has been since Cadolle. The sector’s own consolidation is real but shallow: Van de Velde holds three houses, Calida holds Aubade, Wacoal holds the British bra makers, Chanel holds Eres, and everything else is still on its own.
Two traditions that have converged. The older one is Swiss and German and is about cloth: Zimmerli from 1871, Schiesser from 1875, Hanro from 1884, all making men’s bodywear as a luxury textile product. The newer one, from the 1990s, is about the same design language as women’s lingerie — lace, mesh, sheer, strapping, cut for men and sold as such. Neither is a novelty. Regency dandies wore corsets; Jean Paul Gaultier put men in corsets on a Paris runway from 1984 and never framed it as a joke; Ludovic de Saint Sernin has shown at Paris Fashion Week since 2017 with a collection built almost entirely on the vocabulary.
