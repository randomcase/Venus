New Paris

An outpost on Venus — fifty kilometres above a surface that would crush and melt it, in the one band of this planet where a person could stand outside in shirtsleeves and only need air. no script

Station-keeping altitude the whole trade

Fifty kilometres. Pressure is close to sea level on Earth and the air outside is 75 °C — survivable in a suit, unpleasant, and a constant load on the cooling plant. In exchange the atmosphere is dense and every cubic metre of breathable air holds up more than half a kilogram. Best lift, worst heat.
Fifty-three kilometres. Three quarters of an atmosphere and 50 °C outside. The compromise band: enough density to lift a real structure, cool enough that the radiators are not the whole design. This is where New Paris rides.
Fifty-six kilometres. Half an atmosphere and 20 °C — genuinely shirtsleeve weather, the most Earth-like air anywhere off Earth. But the atmosphere is thin, lift is half what it is down at fifty, and the envelope has to be enormous to hold anything up. Best air, worst lift.

Envelope the habitat is the balloon

Why this works at all. Venus air is 96.5% carbon dioxide — molar mass 44. Breathable nitrogen–oxygen is 29. At the same pressure and temperature the light gas is about two thirds the density of the heavy one, so a volume of air you can live in displaces more than it weighs. There is no separate lifting gas and no separate hull: the room is the balloon. Nowhere else in the solar system is that true.

And the acid. The cloud deck is sulphuric acid droplets. Nothing structural can be bare aluminium or ordinary polymer; the envelope skin is a fluoropolymer over the load-bearing weave, which is well-understood material and the reason the mass figures below are not optimistic.

On the numbers

Lift densities are computed as ρ = PM/RT for each band — carbon dioxide at M = 0.044 against breathable air at M = 0.029 — and the difference rounded to two figures. Pressures and temperatures are standard Venus atmosphere values for those altitudes. They are right to about a significant figure and they are presented that way: enough to show the trade honestly, nowhere near enough to build from. The four-day circulation, the acid cloud composition and the surface conditions are all well-established; the tonnages for decks and plant are my own estimates and should be read as such.

Why an outpost here and not on the ground. Because the ground is 92 bar and 464 °C, and every probe that has reached it has died in about two hours. Fifty kilometres up, the pressure is one bar, the temperature is room temperature, the gravity is 90% of Earth's, and the atmosphere above you is thick enough to stop the radiation that makes Mars difficult. It is the second-best air in the solar system. You just have to stay in it.
