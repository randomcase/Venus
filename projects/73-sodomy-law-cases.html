1. The six steps, with cases

  1. 1533 — England's Buggery Act (25 Hen. 8 c. 6) makes the act a capital offence, the first secular statute in Europe to do so.
    • R v Lord Hungerford, 1540 — Walter Hungerford, Baron Hungerford of Heytesbury, the first man executed under the Act, beheaded with Thomas Cromwell on the same day; buggery was one count among treason.
    • R v Udall, 1541 — Nicholas Udall, headmaster of Eton, confessed to buggery with a pupil; sentence reduced to a year's imprisonment, and he later became headmaster of Westminster.
    • R v Castlehaven, 1631 — Mervyn Touchet, 2nd Earl of Castlehaven, tried by his peers for rape of his wife and sodomy with a servant; beheaded. The case set the rule that the Act reached a peer and did not require proof of emission (later reversed, then restored by statute in 1828).
    • R v Wiseman, 1716 — held that the Act covered the act with a woman as well as a man.
    • R v Pratt and Smith, 1835 — James Pratt and John Smith, hanged at Newgate on 27 November 1835, the last two men executed for the offence in England; observed through a keyhole by a landlord. Dickens saw them in the condemned cell and wrote it up in Sketches by Boz. Both pardoned posthumously in 2017.
    • Offences Against the Person Act 1861 — the death penalty replaced by ten years to life; the first Act to reduce it.
  2. 1860–1900 — the Indian Penal Code's section 377, drafted by Macaulay in 1837 and enacted in 1860, and its copies carry the offence into every British colony; most of today's laws descend from this one clause.
    • Queen-Empress v Khairati, Allahabad High Court, 1884 — the first reported prosecution under 377, of a hijra; conviction quashed for want of evidence of a specific act, but the judgment fixed the reading that "against the order of nature" meant the act itself.
    • Naz Foundation v Government of NCT of Delhi, Delhi High Court, 2009 — read 377 down to exclude consenting adults in private.
    • Suresh Kumar Koushal v Naz Foundation, Supreme Court of India, 2013 — reversed Naz and restored the section, calling the affected population "a minuscule fraction".
    • Navtej Singh Johar v Union of India, Supreme Court of India, 6 September 2018 — five judges unanimously struck 377 as applied to consenting adults, overruling Koushal; the judgment is now cited in the courts of the other former colonies below.
    • Copies: Straits Settlements Penal Code 1871 (later Singapore 377A, 1938), Queensland Criminal Code 1899 (exported to Nigeria, Kenya, Uganda, Tanzania, Papua New Guinea, and the Pacific), the Criminal Code Ordinance 1936 (Palestine, Cyprus), the Sudan Penal Code 1899. The full descent is in the country table below.
  3. 1961 — Illinois becomes the first US state to repeal its sodomy law, on the Model Penal Code's advice.
    • A statute, not a case: the Illinois Criminal Code of 1961, effective 1 January 1962, adopted the American Law Institute's Model Penal Code tentative draft No. 4 (1955), which had recommended removing consensual sodomy from the criminal law.
    • Before it: Rose v Locke (1975) and Wainwright v Stone (1973) later upheld vague "crime against nature" statutes elsewhere; Connecticut was the second state to repeal, in 1969, and twenty states followed in the 1970s.
    • The Wolfenden Report (1957) in Britain, recommending the same, was the document the ALI drafters cited.
  4. 1967 — England and Wales decriminalise, in private, between two men over 21. The exceptions took thirty-six more years to remove.
    • R v Turing, Knutsford Quarter Sessions, 1952 — Alan Turing convicted of gross indecency under section 11 of the Criminal Law Amendment Act 1885, the companion offence to buggery; probation on condition of hormone treatment. Royal pardon 2013; the "Turing law" in the Policing and Crime Act 2017 pardoned about 49,000 others.
    • R v Montagu, Wildeblood and Pitt-Rivers, Winchester Assizes, 1954 — Lord Montagu of Beaulieu, Peter Wildeblood, and Michael Pitt-Rivers imprisoned for offences with two airmen; the public reaction to the trial produced the Wolfenden Committee, whose 1957 report produced the 1967 Act.
    • Sexual Offences Act 1967 — legal only in private, only between two men, only over 21, not in Scotland, Northern Ireland, the armed forces, or the merchant navy. Prosecutions for offences outside these limits rose after 1967.
    • Dudgeon v United Kingdom, European Court of Human Rights, 1981 — the Northern Ireland law breached Article 8; decriminalised there in 1982. Scotland had followed in 1981 by the Criminal Justice (Scotland) Act 1980.
    • Age of consent, 21 to 18: Criminal Justice and Public Order Act 1994, after the Commons rejected 16 by 307 to 280.
    • Sutherland v United Kingdom, European Commission of Human Rights, 1997 — Euan Sutherland's complaint that 18 against 16 for heterosexual sex was discriminatory was upheld; the government undertook to equalise. The Lords blocked the bill twice; the Parliament Acts were used, and the Sexual Offences (Amendment) Act 2000 set 16 from 8 January 2001.
    • Smith and Grady v United Kingdom and Lustig-Prean and Beckett v United Kingdom, ECHR, 1999 — the armed-forces ban breached Article 8; lifted 12 January 2000.
    • ADT v United Kingdom, ECHR, 2000 — the "in private" rule, read to exclude more than two men, breached Article 8; the Sexual Offences Act 2003 then abolished buggery and gross indecency as offences altogether, in force 1 May 2004.
  5. 2003 — Lawrence v Texas strikes down the remaining thirteen US state laws.
    • Bowers v Hardwick, 478 U.S. 186 (1986) — Michael Hardwick, arrested in his Atlanta bedroom by an officer serving a warrant for an unpaid ticket; the Court upheld Georgia's law 5–4, Justice White finding no fundamental right "to engage in homosexual sodomy". Justice Powell later said he had probably made a mistake.
    • Powell v State, Supreme Court of Georgia, 1998 — the same Georgia statute struck under the state constitution's privacy right, twelve years after Bowers had upheld it federally.
    • Commonwealth v Wasson, Kentucky, 1992; Gryczan v State, Montana, 1997; Campbell v Sundquist, Tennessee, 1996 — state courts striking their own laws while Bowers stood.
    • Lawrence v Texas, 539 U.S. 558 (2003) — John Geddes Lawrence and Tyron Garner, arrested in Houston on 17 September 1998 after a false weapons report by a neighbour, fined $200 each under the Texas Homosexual Conduct law. The Court, 6–3, Justice Kennedy writing, overruled Bowers: "Bowers was not correct when it was decided, and it is not correct today." Thirteen state laws fell: Alabama, Florida, Idaho, Kansas, Louisiana, Michigan, Mississippi, Missouri, North Carolina, Oklahoma, South Carolina, Texas, Utah, and Virginia (fourteen by some counts, since Virginia's covered all sodomy).
    • After: several states left the dead statutes on the books; Texas still had its in 2025. Justice Thomas's concurrence in Dobbs v Jackson (2022) named Lawrence as a precedent the Court should "reconsider"; no petition has yet been taken up.
  6. 2018–2025 — India, Botswana, Singapore, Antigua, St Kitts, Barbados, Mauritius, the Cook Islands, Dominica, and Namibia repeal or strike; Trinidad's court puts its law back; Mali, Burkina Faso, and Iraq add new ones. About sixty-four jurisdictions criminalise it between men, twelve retain the death penalty on the books, and in six it has been carried out or is a live sentence. The case reports are below, one row per country.

3. Enacted against enforced

The table's status column says what the statute book says. This section sorts the same 64 jurisdictions by what the state does, from the public record of prosecutions, and the two orderings barely correlate. The penalty on the books predicts almost nothing; Sierra Leone's maximum is life and it has no modern case, Algeria's is two years and it produced the largest mass trial in North Africa in 2020.

Tier 1, enforced most years, about 15. Egypt (through the debauchery law, dozens a year), Tunisia (dozens a year, with forced examinations), Morocco, Algeria (episodic but repeated), Cameroon (every year since 2005), Nigeria (federal and Sharia, mass arrests 2018, 2023), Uganda (steady, sharper since 2023), Tanzania (campaigns 2016–2019), Senegal, Malaysia (selective, and against political enemies), Iran, Saudi Arabia, Yemen under the Houthis, Aceh in Indonesia (public canings since 2017), the Maldives (2022 onward). What these share is not a penalty but a state that gets something from the prosecutions: a morality credential, a distraction, or a political enemy.
Tier 2, sporadic or instrumental, about 25. Kenya, Zambia, Malawi, Zimbabwe, Ghana, the Gambia, Ethiopia, Burundi (one mass conviction), Libya, Sudan, Mauritania (one case), Lebanon, Kuwait, Qatar, the UAE, Turkmenistan, Uzbekistan, Sri Lanka, Myanmar, Bangladesh, Pakistan, Jamaica, Guyana, Grenada, St Lucia, St Vincent, Trinidad. Prosecutions come in ones and twos, often after a complaint by a family member, a video, or a raid on a party; the law's steady use is as a threat, a ground for blackmail by police, and a bar to the registration of any organisation for the group. In the Caribbean there has been essentially no prosecution of consenting adults in decades, and the laws are fought over in court precisely because their effect is civil, not criminal.
Tier 3, furniture, about 20. Kiribati, Tuvalu, Tonga, Samoa, the Solomon Islands, Papua New Guinea, Comoros, Eritrea, Liberia, Sierra Leone, Togo, Guinea, South Sudan, Chad, Oman, Syria, Gaza, Brunei since the moratorium, Eswatini in practice, Somalia's formal code. No reported prosecution of consenting adults in the modern record. The laws are inherited text that no legislature has had a reason to remove and no prosecutor a reason to use. They are not void; see section 4.
Outside the courts, about 4. Al-Shabaab areas of Somalia, Taliban Afghanistan by decree rather than trial, Iraq's militias before and after the 2024 law, Chechnya inside a Russia that does not criminalise. The killing does not depend on the statute and the statute does not restrain it; these are the cases where the table's "law" column is least informative.

4. What "criminalised" means when nobody is prosecuted

The reader asked what it means to criminalise something, and whether an unenforced law is void. The precise answers:

To criminalise

is for a state to declare by law that an act is punishable. It is a statement, made once, by a legislature or a court's reading; it is complete when the text is in force and does not require a prosecution to be true. The table's status column records that statement.

To enforce

is for a prosecutor to bring a case. It is a series of choices made every year by officials with discretion, and section 3 shows it is driven by politics, complaints and campaigns rather than by the text. Most of the 64 states enforce rarely or never.

Desuetude

is the doctrine the reader was reaching for: that a law lapses through long non-use. It exists in Scots law and in a few civil systems, has almost never been applied to a criminal statute anywhere, and is rejected in every common-law system in the table. An unenforced law is dormant, not void; a single complaint revives it, and Cameroon's did in 2005 after decades asleep.

Pre-judgment

is the reader's other word, and it is right in one sense: a criminal statute judges the category in advance, so that the individual case needs only proof of the act. That is what every criminal law does. What distinguishes these is that the act is consensual and private, so the pre-judgment is the whole of the offence.

The four things a dormant law does. It arms the police: a charge that can be threatened produces bribes without a court, and the Nigerian, Kenyan, Ugandan and Cameroonian human-rights records are full of exactly this. It bars the state from serving the group: an NGO cannot register to help criminals (Kenya and Eswatini litigated this for a decade), a clinic cannot advertise PrEP to them, a school cannot mention them, which is why the HIV projections in project 66, section 7, fail worst in these countries. It licenses everyone else: an employer, landlord or family that acts against a person who cannot go to the police. And it waits. Gabon shows the reverse motion too: a state with no law wrote one in 2019 and repealed it in 2020; the statute book is not a settled thing in either direction.

How much do these states care? Unevenly, and the unevenness is the finding. Tier 1 states care actively and periodically, usually when a government needs an enemy or a morality credential; Egypt's 2017 campaign followed a concert, Tanzania's 2018 campaign was one official's programme, Uganda's 2023 Act was passed in a season of anti-Western politics. Tier 2 states care when asked to by a complainant and otherwise not. Tier 3 states have not thought about the question since independence, and the reason the laws survive is that repeal requires a vote nobody wants to hold, not that anyone wants the law. The one thing all three tiers have in common is that "adhering to legality" is beside the point: the law is used where it is useful and ignored where it is not, and the statute's text constrains neither behaviour.

Read the table and the reader's question about penal codes answers itself: the offence is not a feature of Islamic, African, Asian or Caribbean law. It is a feature of one colonial legal tradition, carried in four vehicles, plus a set of national choices made after independence in states whose inherited code was silent, plus the codification of religious law in a dozen states. Where the inherited code was French, Spanish, Portuguese or Dutch, the act is legal today unless a later government chose otherwise, and the list of those that chose otherwise is section 6's subject.

Two regularities. First, the variable that predicts a country's direction is not religion, region or income; it is whether an institution exists that can overrule the executive and whether a claimant can reach it. Muslim-majority Mauritius's court struck its law and Christian-majority Uganda's court upheld a death penalty; the difference is the court. Second, new laws come from governments that are consolidating, not governing: transitional juntas, a ruler pre-empting a boycott, an electorate being offered an enemy. Uganda's Act passed in the same season as its clash with the World Bank; Mali's and Burkina Faso's codes followed their exit from ECOWAS and their break with France; Ghana's bill was the opposition's project and became the government's when the election neared. The laws are about the state's relationship to the outside, and the men and women prosecuted under them are the medium.

7. Algeria, worked

The reader asked for the penal code and the governance of Algeria analysed. Here it is as a template for any row.

The statute

Penal Code, Ordinance 66-156 of 8 June 1966. Article 338: "anyone guilty of a homosexual act" is punished with two months to two years and a fine; if one party is under 18, the adult gets up to three years. Article 333: public indecency, six months to two years, raised to six months to three years and a higher fine "when the outrage to public decency consists of an act against nature with an individual of the same sex". Two articles, one for the act and one for its visibility, and the second is the one usually charged because it does not require proof of the act.

Where it came from

Not from France. The 1810 code Algeria lived under until 1962 had no such offence; the FLN government wrote article 338 into its first national code four years after independence, alongside a family code drawn from Islamic law, as part of defining the new state against the colonial one. It is a post-independence addition in the French lineage, the same move as Senegal in 1965 and Cameroon in 1972.

The constitutional frame

Islam has been the state religion in every Algerian constitution since 1963, including the 2020 text adopted after the Hirak protests, which also guarantees "individual and collective liberties" and the "inviolability of private life". No court has been asked to reconcile the two; the Constitutional Court created in 2020 has no jurisdiction on a private complaint of this kind, and no lawyer has tried. The family code of 1984, amended 2005, does the daily work of defining permitted sexuality; the penal articles are its backstop.

The enforcement record

Sporadic and event-driven. El Tarf, July 2020: 44 people arrested at a private party in a villa, charged under 338 and 333 and with "incitement to debauchery"; two sentenced to two years, most of the rest to suspended terms of one year, on evidence of a rainbow flag and photographs. Before it, cases in Tizi Ouzou and Constantine in ones and twos, mostly after family complaints. No annual count exists because there is no steady enforcement to count; Algeria is a tier 1 state by episode and a tier 2 state by year.

Governance

A presidential republic in which the army has chosen every president; the Hirak movement of 2019 removed one and the system survived. The state uses morality legislation the way section 6 describes: as a marker of its relationship to Islam and to the former coloniser, not as a programme. It has not hardened the law since 1966, has not been pressed to repeal it, votes against every UN resolution on the subject, and permits no organisation to register for the group. The two-year maximum understates what "criminalised" means here; the absence of any registered organisation, clinic outreach or press coverage is the law's actual effect, and it costs nothing to maintain.

What would change the row

A constitutional petition on the 2020 private-life clause, which no one has brought; a family-code reform, which is the argument Algerian feminists have been having since 1984; or nothing, which is the likeliest, since a dormant law that occasionally produces a trial serves every interest the state has. The El Tarf defendants were released within the year. The article is now sixty years old.
