Page 1 of 10 — The Buggery Act

25 Henry VIII c. 6, 1533: the first secular statute in Europe to make the act a capital felony, passed while Henry was stripping the church courts of jurisdiction. Hungerford beheaded 1540; Castlehaven 1631; Pratt and Smith hanged 1835; death replaced by ten years to life in 1861.

Exercises

  1. The Act was passed in 1533 and the last hanging was 1835. Name the political reason a Tudor parliament wanted this offence in a secular court.
    answer

    It transferred jurisdiction from the church courts to the Crown's, in the year Henry broke with Rome. The Act was one of a series moving offences the church had tried into the king's courts, and its first victim, Hungerford, was convicted alongside charges of treason. It was about who judged, before it was about what.

  2. Castlehaven, 1631, was tried by his peers for two offences. Which two, and what rule about evidence did the case set?
    answer

    Rape of his wife (by procuring a servant) and sodomy with a servant. The court held that penetration alone, without proof of emission, sufficed; later courts reversed that, and the 1828 Offences Against the Person Act restored it by statute.

  3. Pratt and Smith were seen through a keyhole. Who saw them, who saw them afterwards, and what happened in 2017?
    answer

    Their landlord, who fetched the police. Charles Dickens visited them in Newgate's condemned cell and described them in Sketches by Boz. Both were pardoned under the Policing and Crime Act 2017, along with about 49,000 others convicted under the buggery and gross indecency laws.

  4. Why does Alan Turing belong on this page even though he was not convicted under the Buggery Act?
    answer

    He was convicted in 1952 of gross indecency under section 11 of the Criminal Law Amendment Act 1885, the companion offence added to catch acts short of buggery. The two laws were repealed together in 1967 and the pardon of 2017 covers both.

Page 2 of 10 — Section 377

Drafted by Macaulay in 1837, enacted in the Indian Penal Code of 1860: "carnal intercourse against the order of nature", life imprisonment. Copied into the Straits Settlements code (1871), the Queensland code (1899, exported to East Africa, Nigeria and the Pacific), the Sudan code (1899), and the Palestine and Cyprus ordinance (1936). Struck in India by Navtej Singh Johar, 2018.

Exercises

  1. Put the four Indian cases in order with court and result.
    answer

    Khairati, Allahabad High Court 1884, conviction quashed but the reading fixed. Naz Foundation, Delhi High Court 2009, section read down. Koushal, Supreme Court 2013, restored. Navtej Singh Johar, Supreme Court 2018, struck, five to nothing.

  2. Macaulay refused to define the offence in his notes, saying he was unwilling to insert "either the definition or the illustrations". What did that refusal do for the next 150 years?
    answer

    It left "against the order of nature" to the courts, which read it to cover anal and oral sex between any persons, and later to cover only same-sex acts in practice. The vagueness was what let Navtej Johar strike it for consenting adults without touching its use against non-consensual acts.

  3. Trace one clause from Whitehall to a Pacific island. Name the codes it passed through.
    answer

    Indian Penal Code 1860 → Queensland Criminal Code 1899 (Griffith's code, drawn partly on the Indian model) → adopted for Papua 1902 and New Guinea 1921 → Papua New Guinea Criminal Code 1974, sections 210 and 212, still in force. The Solomon Islands, Kiribati and Tuvalu took the same sections from the Western Pacific High Commission's code.

  4. Singapore's 377A was different from India's 377. How, and what did that do to its repeal?
    answer

    377A (1938) criminalised "gross indecency" between men specifically, modelled on the English 1885 Act, while 377 covered "carnal intercourse against the order of nature" for anyone. Singapore repealed 377 in 2007 and kept 377A; three challenges failed on the ground that Parliament had chosen it deliberately; Parliament repealed it in 2022 while amending the constitution to protect the definition of marriage, so the courts could not go further.

Page 3 of 10 — The exceptions, 1967–2004

The Sexual Offences Act 1967 legalised the act in private between two men over 21 in England and Wales. Scotland 1981, Northern Ireland 1982 after Dudgeon; age 18 in 1994; the armed forces 2000 after Smith and Grady; age 16 in 2001 after Sutherland; "in private" widened after ADT in 2000; the offences abolished by the Sexual Offences Act 2003.

Exercises

  1. How many years from partial decriminalisation to the abolition of the offence, and how many separate exceptions had to be removed?
    answer

    1967 to 2004, thirty-seven years. Six: Scotland, Northern Ireland, the armed forces and merchant navy, the age of 21 then 18, the two-person rule for "in private", and finally the existence of buggery and gross indecency as offences at all.

  2. Prosecutions for gross indecency rose after 1967. Explain how a decriminalisation can increase convictions.
    answer

    The Act legalised only a narrow case and by defining it made everything outside it, sex with a man under 21, sex with a third person present, sex in a hotel room or a park, clearly criminal and worth prosecuting. Convictions for gross indecency roughly doubled between 1967 and 1989 as police pursued the boundaries the Act had drawn.

  3. Which court forced each of the following, and in what year: Northern Ireland, the armed forces, the age of 16?
    answer

    Northern Ireland: European Court of Human Rights, Dudgeon v UK, 1981. Armed forces: European Court, Smith and Grady v UK and Lustig-Prean and Beckett v UK, 1999. Age of 16: the European Commission of Human Rights, Sutherland v UK, 1997, on which the government undertook to legislate; the Lords blocked it twice and the Parliament Acts were used in 2000.

Page 4 of 10 — Bowers to Lawrence

Bowers v Hardwick, 1986: Georgia's law upheld 5–4. State courts struck their own laws through the 1990s (Wasson, Kentucky 1992; Campbell, Tennessee 1996; Gryczan, Montana 1997; Powell, Georgia 1998). Lawrence v Texas, 2003: Bowers overruled 6–3, thirteen state laws fell.

Exercises

  1. Georgia's law was upheld by the US Supreme Court in 1986 and struck by the Georgia Supreme Court in 1998. How can both be right?
    answer

    Bowers held the federal constitution did not protect the conduct; Powell held Georgia's own constitution did. A state court may read its constitution to give more protection than the federal floor, and cannot be reversed on that by the federal court.

  2. Lawrence and Garner were arrested on a false report. Whose, and why does the falsity matter to the case?
    answer

    A neighbour, Robert Eubanks, called in a false weapons disturbance; he was later convicted of filing a false report. It matters because the police entry was lawful on the report, so the case could not be won on the search; it had to be won on the conduct itself, which is what made it the vehicle to overrule Bowers.

  3. Name the thirteen laws that fell, and say why some count fourteen.
    answer

    Alabama, Florida, Idaho, Kansas, Louisiana, Michigan, Mississippi, Missouri, North Carolina, Oklahoma, South Carolina, Texas, Utah, and Virginia. Four (Kansas, Oklahoma, Texas, Missouri) applied only to same-sex conduct, nine to all sodomy; Virginia's is the fourteenth in most lists, and thirteen is the number Kennedy's opinion gave.

  4. What did Justice Thomas write in 2022, in which case, and what has happened since?
    answer

    Concurring in Dobbs v Jackson, he wrote that the Court should "reconsider" Griswold, Lawrence and Obergefell as substantive-due-process precedents. No petition asking the Court to do so has been granted; Texas's statute remains on its books unenforced.

Page 5 of 10 — The savings clause

Most Caribbean constitutions written at independence contain a clause preserving pre-independence laws from constitutional challenge. It decided Tomlinson in Jamaica (2023, dismissed) and the Trinidad Court of Appeal's reversal of Jones (2025). Courts in Belize, Antigua, St Kitts, Barbados and Dominica found ways round it or had none to face.

Exercises

  1. State what a general savings clause does in one sentence.
    answer

    It provides that a law in force on independence day cannot be held inconsistent with the constitution's bill of rights, so the rights apply to new laws and not to the old ones.

  2. Trinidad's High Court struck the law in 2018 and its Court of Appeal restored it in 2025. What did the appeal court say about the savings clause, and what did it do to the penalty?
    answer

    That the Sexual Offences Act 1986, although passed after independence, re-enacted a saved colonial offence and inherited its protection, so the court could not strike it. It reduced the maximum from 25 years to 5, treating the increase as the only unsaved part. The case is on its way to the Privy Council.

  3. Why did the same clause not stop the courts in Belize and Antigua?
    answer

    Belize's savings clause had expired: it protected existing laws only for five years after independence. Antigua's 1995 Sexual Offences Act was a post-independence statute that the court held was not a mere re-enactment. Where the clause is permanent and the law is old, as in Jamaica, the challenge fails; the outcome depends on a drafting choice made in the 1960s.

Page 6 of 10 — Africa's two directions

Since 2015: repealed or struck in Mozambique, Seychelles, Botswana, Angola, Gabon, Mauritius, Namibia (on appeal). New or harsher laws in Chad, Gabon (briefly), Uganda, Ghana (bill), Mali, Burkina Faso. Courts upheld the laws in Kenya (2019) and Malawi (2024).

Exercises

  1. Sort the repeals into "by a court" and "by a legislature". What does the split tell you?
    answer

    Court: South Africa 1998, Botswana 2019, Mauritius 2023, Namibia 2024. Legislature: Mozambique 2015, Seychelles 2016, Angola 2021, Gabon 2020. The legislative repeals came with wholesale new penal codes in former Portuguese colonies and the Seychelles; the court repeals came in common-law states with strong constitutional courts. Nobody repealed by a vote on the question alone.

  2. Uganda's 2014 Act was annulled and its 2023 Act was upheld. What was the difference?
    answer

    The 2014 Act was struck on procedure: Parliament lacked a quorum when it passed. The 2023 Act was passed with a quorum and challenged on substance, and the Constitutional Court upheld all but four clauses in April 2024, which is the first time a court on the continent has upheld a law of this severity on its merits.

  3. Mali and Burkina Faso had never criminalised the act in their history and did so in 2024 and 2025. Name what the two governments have in common.
    answer

    Both are military transitional governments installed by coups (2020–21 and 2022), both left ECOWAS in 2025, both have broken with France and turned to Russia, and both passed the laws through appointed transitional assemblies as part of a programme of "sovereignty" and "values" legislation. Page 10 is about this.

Page 7 of 10 — Death on the books

Twelve jurisdictions retain a death penalty for the act: Iran, Saudi Arabia, Yemen, Afghanistan, Mauritania, Brunei, Qatar, the UAE, Nigeria's northern states, Somalia in areas, Uganda for "aggravated homosexuality", and Pakistan under the Hudood Ordinances. In six it is carried out, has been recently, or is a live sentence.

Exercises

  1. Divide the twelve into "executed or live sentence" and "on the books only", and give the evidence for one of each.
    answer

    Live: Iran (2005 Mashhad hangings; executions in most years since), Saudi Arabia (among the 37 executed 23 April 2019), Yemen (13 death sentences in Ibb, 2024), Afghanistan (Sharia by decree since 2021, floggings recorded), Somalia (al-Shabaab), northern Nigeria (sentences passed, none carried out). On the books only: Mauritania (no execution since independence), Brunei (moratorium 2019), Qatar and the UAE (no recorded execution for it), Pakistan (Hudood never applied for this), Uganda (first charge 2023, no sentence).

  2. Yemen's 2024 sentences were passed by which authority, and why does that complicate the country row?
    answer

    Courts in Ibb under Houthi control, not the internationally recognised government in Aden. The row records the state's 1994 Penal Code, which both sides apply; the enforcement is by a de facto authority the UN does not recognise, which is also true of Afghanistan and parts of Somalia.

  3. Brunei announced a moratorium within a month of the law taking full effect. What moved it, and what did not change?
    answer

    A boycott of the Sultan's hotels and the withdrawal of several universities' honours in April–May 2019. The provisions of the Syariah Penal Code Order remain in force; a moratorium is a promise by the head of state, not a repeal.

Page 8 of 10 — Enacted, enforced, furniture

Of about 64 criminalising jurisdictions, roughly 15 prosecute the act itself every year, roughly 25 prosecute sporadically or use the law for other ends, and roughly 20 have no recorded modern prosecution. In a further group the killing is done outside any court. A law that is never enforced still does four things.

Exercises

  1. Name the four things an unenforced sodomy law does.
    answer

    It gives police a charge to threaten and extort with. It blocks the registration of organisations, the licensing of clinics, and the distribution of condoms and PrEP to the group the law names, because the state cannot fund a service to criminals. It lets employers, landlords and families act with impunity, since the victim cannot complain. And it is available: Cameroon's article was dormant until 2005 and has been used every year since.

  2. "De facto void" is a phrase people reach for. Is there a legal doctrine that a law lapses through non-use, and does any of these countries apply it?
    answer

    Desuetude: the doctrine that a statute long unenforced loses its force. It exists in Scots law and in a few civil-law systems and has almost never been applied by a court to a criminal statute; no common-law country accepts it. The Pacific laws are furniture in practice, not void in law, and a new government or a single complaint can revive any of them, which Gabon did in reverse in 2019 by enacting one.

  3. Egypt has no law against the act and prosecutes it more than almost anyone. Explain.
    answer

    It uses the 1961 law against "habitual debauchery", written for prostitution, and the courts read consensual sex between men as debauchery. Forensic anal examinations, which have no diagnostic value, are ordered as evidence. Egypt is the proof that the statute matters less than the will to prosecute, and Lebanon, where a court read the statute not to apply and prosecutions continue anyway, is the same proof from the other side.

  4. Which is the better predictor of a prosecution in a given year: the penalty on the books, or something else? Name the something else.
    answer

    Something else: whether the government is using the group for political purposes that year. Tanzania 2018, Egypt 2017, Nigeria 2018 and 2023, Uganda 2023, Ghana 2021, Indonesia 2017 were each a campaign with arrests, not a steady enforcement of a penalty. The penalty predicts almost nothing; Sierra Leone's is life and it has no modern case.

Page 9 of 10 — Where the codes came from

Five lineages produce almost every law in the table: the British (IPC 377, the Queensland code, the 1861 Act, common-law sodomy); Islamic law codified or uncodified; the Soviet article of 1934 (Uzbekistan 120, Turkmenistan 135); post-independence additions in former French colonies whose inherited code had no such offence; and a handful of home-grown recent statutes. Two lineages produce none: the Napoleonic code as inherited, and the Spanish and Portuguese codes of the 19th century.

Exercises

  1. France decriminalised in 1791. Senegal, Cameroon, Algeria, Tunisia, Morocco, Guinea, Chad, Mali and Burkina Faso all criminalise. Reconcile.
    answer

    None of them inherited the offence from the French code. Each added it after independence: Tunisia in 1913 under the protectorate's own code, Morocco 1962, Senegal 1965, Algeria 1966, Cameroon 1972 by presidential decree, Guinea 2016, Chad 2017, Mali 2024, Burkina Faso 2025. The French lineage explains why Côte d'Ivoire, Gabon (until 2019), Benin, Niger, Congo and Madagascar do not criminalise; the exceptions are national choices, mostly with religion cited.

  2. Why does almost no Latin American country criminalise, while almost every Anglophone Caribbean island does?
    answer

    Spain's 1822 penal code, following the French, omitted the offence, and the new republics copied Spanish and French models; Brazil's 1830 code did the same. The Caribbean islands took the English Offences Against the Person Act 1861 and kept it at independence behind savings clauses. The dividing line in the table is the language of the colonial power.

  3. Uzbekistan and Turkmenistan criminalise; Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan and Tajikistan do not. All five inherited the same Soviet article. What happened?
    answer

    Russia repealed article 121 in 1993 and three of the Central Asian republics followed in their new codes of 1997–98. Uzbekistan and Turkmenistan, the two most closed states of the five, kept theirs, and Uzbekistan chose to retain it again when redrafting in 2021. Same inheritance, different governments.

  4. Indonesia never had a national law, and from 2026 will have one that criminalises all sex outside marriage. Which lineage is that, and who does it reach?
    answer

    Home-grown, with a religious rationale, in the 2023 Criminal Code replacing the Dutch colonial code of 1918 which, like the French, had no such offence. It criminalises extramarital sex on the complaint of a spouse, parent or child, which reaches same-sex couples because they cannot marry, without naming them.

Page 10 of 10 — Governance

Of the criminalising jurisdictions, almost none is a liberal democracy with an independent constitutional court; of the repeals since 2015, almost all came from such courts or from wholesale code reform in states seeking European or investor approval. New laws since 2019 came from military transitional governments, theocracies, or elected governments campaigning against "Western values".

Exercises

  1. Algeria: identify the statute, the year it was added, the constitutional context, and the enforcement pattern, in four sentences.
    answer

    Articles 338 (homosexuality, two months to two years) and 333 (public indecency, aggravated to three years if same-sex) of the Penal Code, Ordinance 66-156 of 1966, four years after independence; the French code Algeria inherited had no such offence. Islam is the state religion under every constitution since 1963, including the 2020 text, and morality law is justified from it. Enforcement is sporadic and event-driven: the El Tarf party of 2020 (44 arrested, two sentenced to two years, most given suspended terms) is the largest case in a decade, in a country of 45 million, and there is no annual prosecution count because there are few. The law's daily work is done by police discretion and by the absence of any registered organisation or health service for the group, which is what "criminalised" means in Algeria.

  2. Take the countries that struck or repealed since 2015 and the countries that enacted since 2017. Describe the typical government in each group.
    answer

    Repealed or struck: multiparty states with courts that have struck other laws recently (Botswana, India, Mauritius, Namibia, the Eastern Caribbean), or presidential states rewriting a whole colonial code with European technical assistance (Mozambique, Angola, Seychelles). Enacted: military transitional governments (Mali, Burkina Faso, Chad's code came a year before its own upheaval), a theocracy (Brunei), a religious-populist coalition (Iraq 2024), and an elected government using the law as a foreign-policy position (Uganda, Ghana's bill). The variable is not religion or region; it is whether anyone can overrule the executive.

  3. "Criminalise" is used in the table as a status. Write a definition of it that fits Egypt, Sierra Leone, Uganda and Tuvalu at once.
    answer

    A state criminalises the act when its law makes the act itself, or a category that its courts read to include the act, punishable, whether or not anyone is punished. Egypt fits by the second clause, Sierra Leone and Tuvalu by the first without enforcement, Uganda by both. The status says what the state has said; pages 8 and 10 say what it does.

  4. Predict, for one country in the table, what changes its row in the next five years, and name the mechanism.
    answer

    Any defensible answer names a mechanism. Examples: Trinidad, the Privy Council's ruling on the savings clause, which would also bind Jamaica's reasoning; Namibia, the Supreme Court's decision on the state's appeal; Sri Lanka, a parliamentary vote on the bill the Supreme Court has already cleared; Kenya, the Court of Appeal's judgment in EG, pending since 2019; Uganda, the Supreme Court appeal, or a change of president; Indonesia, the first prosecution under the 2026 code; Mali or Burkina Faso, the end of the transitional government, in either direction.
