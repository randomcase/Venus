Zero transmissions: the PARTNER studies

Project 68 · a report on one sentence in project 66 · index · repo randomcase/zero-transmissions-the-partner-studies

The question. Project 66 says an HIV-positive partner with a suppressed viral load "transmitted zero infections in about 77,000 condomless acts". A reader asked what happened the 77,000th time, and whether they shot the cameraman. The short answer is that there was no cameraman, no 77,000th time, and the number is not quite right. This report says what the number was, who counted it, how, what zero can mean when nobody was watching, and what happened to the fifteen men in the study who did get HIV.

Add PARTNER1 and Opposites Attract and the count of condomless acts under suppression with zero linked transmissions is about 150,000, of which about 93,000 were anal.

2. Who counted, and how

No one watched.

The count is a sum of questionnaires. At each clinic visit, every four to six months, the HIV-negative partner was asked how many times since the last visit the couple had had sex without a condom, of which kinds, and whether there was ejaculation inside. The positive partner had a viral load test at the same visit. The acts were never observed, timed, or verified by anyone; the study trusted the diaries and said so.

What counted as eligible.

A couple-year of follow-up went into the analysis only if the positive partner's most recent viral load was under 200 copies per millilitre, the negative partner was not on PrEP or post-exposure prophylaxis, and the couple reported condomless sex in the period. Follow-up that failed any of these was set aside, which is why 972 couples enrolled became 782 analysed.

How zero was established.

Every HIV-negative partner was tested for HIV at each visit. When one tested positive, the virus from both partners was sequenced (the pol and env genes) and compared. If the two sequences were close enough to be the same lineage, the transmission was "linked" and would have counted. None were. Fifteen were "unlinked": a different virus, from a different person.

What 76,088 acts looks like.

782 couples over 1,593 couple-years is about 48 condomless acts per couple per year, just under one a week. Not a marathon. Two years of ordinary sex in eight hundred ordinary relationships, written down at the clinic.

3. What zero can mean when nobody was watching

Zero events in a finite sample does not prove a rate of zero. It puts a ceiling on it. The rule of thumb is the rule of three: if you observe nothing in n trials, the true rate is below about 3/n with 95% confidence; the exact Poisson figure is 3.69/n for the one-sided bound the study used.

PARTNER2: 3.69 / 1,593 couple-years = 0.23 per 100 couple-years, which is the number the paper reports.
Per act: 3.69 / 76,088 = 0.49 per 10,000 acts, against 138 per 10,000 for an untreated partner.
Receptive anal sex with ejaculation, the highest-risk subset: 0 events in ~570 couple-years, upper bound about 0.57 per 100 couple-years.

So the honest statement is not "the risk is zero". It is "the risk is below one in twenty thousand acts, and every one of about 150,000 acts across three studies produced nothing, while the same design detected fifteen infections from outside the couples without difficulty". The public-health slogan, undetectable equals untransmittable, is the ceiling rounded to the word people will remember. The Swiss Federal Commission said the same thing in 2008 on much less evidence and was attacked for it; PARTNER is the evidence they did not yet have.

4. What happened the fifteen times

Fifteen HIV-negative men in PARTNER2 tested positive during follow-up, and eleven people in PARTNER1. Nothing happened to a cameraman. In every case the virus was sequenced, compared with the study partner's, and found to be a different lineage. In interviews, the men reported condomless sex with other partners, which the study design expected: the couples were not asked to be monogamous, only to report. The infections are the study's control, in effect. They show that the testing found infections when they occurred, and that the only ones it found came from partners who were not suppressed.

The reader's version.

The 77,000th act did not exist. Acts were counted at the resolution of "since your last visit", so there is no first, no last, and no ordinal 77,000; there is a sum of about eight hundred columns of small numbers, and a row of HIV tests beside them that never turned up the study partner's virus.

The sceptic's version.

The men might have under-reported condom use, over-reported condomless acts, or forgotten. They might. Under-reporting condomless sex would make the ceiling too generous, over-reporting would make it too strict; there is no reason to think the errors run one way, and the outside infections show the negative partners were not shy about sex the study had not asked them to have.

5. What the studies could not say

  • The first months of treatment. A viral load takes weeks to months to fall below 200 after treatment starts; the studies enrolled couples already suppressed and excluded periods that were not. The zero applies after suppression, not after the first pill. HPTN 052's eight on-treatment infections are almost all from that window or from treatment failure.
  • Blips and lapses. Viral load was tested every few months. Between tests it could have risen, and some couple-years were excluded for exactly that reason. Suppression is a state that has to be maintained and checked; the zero belongs to the couples who checked.
  • Other infections. A suppressed viral load stops HIV. It does nothing about gonorrhoea, chlamydia, syphilis, hepatitis, or HPV, all of which were common in the cohort and none of which the result addresses. Roughly a third of couple-years in PARTNER2 included a reported STI in one partner, and the HIV result was unchanged by it.
  • Anyone outside a couple. These were stable pairs who knew each other's status. The result is about the suppressed partner's virus; it transfers to any suppressed partner, but a stranger's word that they are suppressed is not a viral load test.

6. Why it matters more than most trials

PARTNER2 is the reason the treatment-as-prevention row in project 66 sits at the top. It changed criminal law: several jurisdictions that prosecuted HIV non-disclosure now exempt suppressed defendants because of it. It changed what clinicians tell serodifferent couples who want children, which had been the original reason people asked the question. And it moved the aim of treatment from the patient alone to the patient and everyone they sleep with, which is where the projections in project 66, section 7, come from and why the missed second 95 in 95-95-95 costs so much.
