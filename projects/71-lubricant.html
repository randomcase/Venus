Lubricant technology

Project 71 · a report on one card in project 66, in depth · index · repo randomcase/lubricant-technology

The question. Project 66 says: water- or silicone-based, plenty of it, under about 1,200 mOsm/kg, no nonoxynol-9. The reader asked what is going on in lubricant technology. Quite a lot, most of it in the last twenty years and most of it driven by HIV research rather than by the products people buy. This report covers the physics of why the rectum needs it, the chemistry of what is in the bottle, osmolality and pH with the actual measurements, the cell and tissue studies that produced the WHO advisory, the microbicide programme that tried to make lubricant carry a drug, what the regulators require, and what is being built now.

1. Why the rectum needs it: the tribology

Friction between two wet, soft surfaces is governed by the fluid film between them. With enough fluid and enough speed the surfaces ride on the film and never touch (hydrodynamic lubrication, friction coefficient near 0.01). As the film thins, or the motion slows, the surfaces touch through it (mixed, then boundary lubrication) and friction rises ten- to a hundredfold. The vagina supplies its own film: transudate from the walls and mucus from the cervix, produced within a minute of arousal, plus a lining a dozen cells thick that can lose its top layers without bleeding. The rectum supplies a thin mucus for the passage of stool, nothing on arousal, and a lining one cell thick over a dense capillary bed. Without an added film, the rectum is in the boundary regime from the first stroke, and the surface that gives is the epithelium.

Two properties of the fluid decide how far right you get and how long you stay there. Viscosity and its behaviour under shear: a good lubricant is shear-thinning, thick at rest so it stays put, thin in motion so it flows into the gap; the body's mucus does this through long glycoprotein chains, and synthetic lubricants copy it with polymers. Persistence: water-based lubricants lose water to the tissue and the air and thicken into a tacky film within minutes; silicone lubricants do not evaporate or absorb and last until wiped off. This is the entire practical case for silicone in anal sex, and the entire practical case against it is that it stains, does not wash off with water, and degrades silicone toys.

2. What is in the bottle

Waterthe solvent in water-based products, 60–90%
Glycerin, propylene glycol, sorbitolhumectants: hold water, give slip, taste sweet, and are the reason for hyperosmolality. Glycerin at 10–30% by weight puts a lubricant at 2,000–6,000 mOsm/kg on its own. Also feed yeast.
Polymershydroxyethylcellulose (HEC), carbomer (polyacrylic acid), polyethylene glycol, xanthan, guar. Thicken and give the shear-thinning body; HEC at 2–3% is the base of the "universal placebo" gel used in HIV trials because it is nearly inert.
Siliconesdimethicone, cyclopentasiloxane, dimethiconol, cyclomethicone. Inert, hydrophobic, no osmolality (no water), do not absorb; a few drops last. Incompatible with silicone toys, compatible with latex.
Preservativesmethyl- and propylparaben, phenoxyethanol, potassium sorbate, sodium benzoate, chlorhexidine gluconate. Required in anything with water. Chlorhexidine was the most toxic to cells in the laboratory panels; parabens are debated and being removed for marketing reasons more than evidence.
pH adjusters and bufferscitric acid, lactic acid, sodium hydroxide. Set the pH; lactic acid buffers to the vaginal range.
Actives, so calledbenzocaine or lidocaine (numbing), menthol or capsaicin (warming and cooling), L-arginine, aloe, hyaluronic acid, CBD, "probiotics". The first two are the ones that matter, and not in a good way; see section 5.
Oilscoconut, almond, mineral oil, petroleum jelly, shea. Excellent lubricants, safe for tissue, destroy latex and polyisoprene within a minute, hard to clear, and associated with bacterial vaginosis when used vaginally.
Nonoxynol-9a surfactant spermicide. Dissolves cell membranes, including the ones lining the rectum. Excluded from every guideline for anal use since 2002.

3. Osmolality and pH: the measurements

Osmolality is the concentration of dissolved particles per kilogram of water. Cells sit in fluid at about 290 mOsm/kg. Put them in a fluid several times more concentrated and water leaves them to equalise; they shrink, detach, and die. The rectal lining is one cell thick, so the effect is a bare capillary bed within the hour. This is the mechanism the WHO advisory of 2012 is built on, and the measurements are below.

pH. The vagina sits at 3.8–4.5, maintained by lactobacilli producing lactic acid; a lubricant far above that disturbs the flora and the WHO recommends about 4.5 for vaginal products. The rectum sits near neutral, 7–8, and a lubricant made for the vagina is mildly acidic for it, which the tissue tolerates better than the reverse. Almost no product states either number on the label; the WHO advisory asks procurers to demand both.

Arithmetic from project 67: a 10,000 mOsm/kg gel is about 34 times body osmolality. A 1,200 mOsm/kg gel is about 4 times, which the tissue studies tolerated with minor changes; the WHO set the ceiling there as a compromise with what manufacturers could make cheaply, and said 380 is what they should aim at.

The picture from the bench is consistent and the picture from the population is thin. Nobody has run a trial of iso-osmolar against hyperosmolar lubricant with HIV or STI acquisition as the outcome, and nobody will, so the WHO advisory rests on tissue and mechanism. It is nonetheless the best-founded product advice in project 66, because the mechanism is visible through a sigmoidoscope an hour after application.

5. The actives, and the numbing problem

Warming lubricants work by hygroscopy: a high concentration of glycerin or propylene glycol pulls water from tissue and releases heat, which is to say the warming sensation is the osmotic damage in section 3 felt as it happens. Cooling products use menthol; both are irritants at the concentrations used. Numbing products carry benzocaine at up to 5% or lidocaine, and are marketed specifically for anal sex. Project 66, sections 2 and 4, is the case against them: pain during anal sex is the internal sphincter refusing and the lining tearing, and a product that removes the signal removes the only thing that stops the damage. No clinical guide recommends them; several warn against them; the market for them is large.

6. The microbicide programme: lubricant as a drug

From about 1995 to 2016 several hundred million dollars went into gels that a woman could apply before sex to block HIV, and from 2007 the same idea was tried rectally. Lubricant science is largely a by-product of that programme.

Three things the programme left behind. The HEC placebo gel, the first lubricant designed to do nothing, now the reference iso-osmolar formulation. The osmolality science of section 3, learned because the first rectal gel caused cramps. And the Fuchs and Dezzutti product panels, which are why the WHO advisory exists and why the labels are starting to change.

7. Regulation

  • United States. Personal lubricants are Class II medical devices (21 CFR 884.5300), cleared by 510(k) on equivalence to an existing product, with ISO 10993 biocompatibility testing including a rabbit vaginal irritation test, and condom compatibility under ASTM D7661. Osmolality and pH are not required on the label, and most 510(k) clearances were granted without them. A product sold as a cosmetic or "massage gel" avoids even that.
  • Europe. Medical devices under the MDR since 2021, Class IIa or IIb; the same silence on osmolality.
  • WHO / UNFPA / FHI 360 advisory note, 2012. Not a regulation; a procurement standard for the agencies that buy condoms and lubricant for distribution. Osmolality under 1,200 mOsm/kg, ideally near 380; pH about 4.5 for vaginal use; no nonoxynol-9; no chlorhexidine; disclosed ingredients. It is the only document in the world that sets a number, and it applies only to what the agencies buy.
  • ISO 19671:2018. "Additional lubricants for male natural rubber latex condoms": test methods for compatibility, viscosity, pH and osmolality, without limits. The limits are the advisory's.

8. What is being built

Iso-osmolar as a category.

Since about 2015 a segment of the market has reformulated around the WHO numbers and says so on the bottle: glycerin-free, 250–400 mOsm/kg, pH stated. It is the first time the advisory has reached a shelf, and it happened through marketing rather than regulation.

Mucin mimics.

The body's lubricant is mucin, a bottlebrush glycoprotein that binds water along a stiff backbone. Synthetic bottlebrush polymers that copy it (Cornell, 2021 onward) match its lubricity at a fraction of the concentration and do not thin as water is lost; they are the first lubricant chemistry that is not a humectant, a polymer thickener, or a silicone.

Hyaluronic acid.

Borrowed from joint injections and dermatology; holds water without the osmotic load of glycerin and is being trialled as a moisturiser for vaginal atrophy with results comparable to oestrogen cream. Its rectal behaviour is unstudied.

Self-lubricating surfaces.

The hydrophilic condom coating in project 70, which turns the condom into the lubricant for a thousand strokes; and hydrogel condoms that are lubricant all the way through.

Drug carriers, again.

The tenofovir douche and the microbicide gels of section 6 are lubricant technology by another name: a vehicle designed to be applied where sex happens, with a drug in it. Whether any reaches market depends on whether a twice-yearly injection leaves them a reason to exist.

Microbiome-aware products.

Lubricants buffered with lactic acid or seeded with lactobacilli, aimed at the bacterial-vaginosis association of the hyperosmolar products; small trials, no rectal equivalent, though the rectal microbiome's role in HIV susceptibility (project 66, section 6) makes one likely.

9. What the card in project 66 should have said, in full

Silicone if you want persistence and are not using silicone toys; water-based if you want it to wash off and are prepared to reapply, in which case glycerin-free and under about 400 mOsm/kg if the label says, under 1,200 if it does not. Never oil with latex or polyisoprene. Never nonoxynol-9. Never a numbing product. Warming means damage. Plenty of it, on the outside of the condom and on the tissue, and again when it dries, because the whole point is to stay on the right side of the curve in section 1.
