Condoms: the 70%, and the projections

Project 70 · a report on one sentence in project 66 · index · repo randomcase/condoms-the-70-and-the-projections

The question. Project 66 says consistent condom use "reduces HIV transmission through anal sex by about 70%, less than the often-quoted vaginal figure, because breakage and slippage are more common". The reader asked for the projections. This report gives where the 70% and the 80% come from, how condoms fail and how often, what has happened to condom use since PrEP arrived, what the models say about HIV and the other infections as it falls further, and what the object is made of and might be made of next.

1. Where the two numbers come from

Neither is a laboratory figure. In the laboratory a latex condom is impermeable to HIV, which is about 0.1 micrometres across, and to every other sexual pathogen; water leak tests on 300,000 condoms a batch find holes in a fraction of a percent. The 80% and 70% are what condoms achieved in the hands of people who said they always used them, measured against people who said they never did, in cohorts followed for HIV.

The gap between 70% and 95% is the whole subject. It is made of three things: condoms that break or slip, condoms that go on late or come off early, and the fact that people who report "always" do not always mean always. The anal figure is lower than the vaginal one on all three counts.

2. How condoms fail, and how often

  • Breakage. Higher during anal sex because of friction against a lining that does not lubricate, and because of longer or more vigorous acts. Adding lubricant halves breakage in every study that measured it; oil-based lubricant raises it several-fold by weakening latex within minutes. Thicker "extra strong" condoms did not reduce breakage in the one randomised trial (Golombok 1997, 283 gay men): lubrication and fit mattered, thickness did not.
  • Slippage. Off during withdrawal, or off inside. More common with too much lubricant inside the condom, poor fit, and loss of erection, all of which are more frequent in anal sex.
  • Late and early. Studies that ask find 15–40% of "condom-protected" acts began before the condom went on or continued after it came off. These acts are counted as protected in the cohorts that produced the 70%.
  • Not used. "Always" in a questionnaire over six months means "I intend to and mostly do". This is not a condom failure; it is why the population figure will never reach the laboratory figure.
Arithmetic: a perfect barrier with a 3% per-act failure rate, on the 138 per 10,000 baseline, leaves 138 × 0.03 ≈ 4 per 10,000, a 97% reduction. The observed 70% therefore means condoms in the cohorts were not on for roughly a quarter to a third of the acts the participants called protected, or failed more than they noticed, or both.

3. What has happened to use

Among men who have sex with men in the United States, the share reporting any condomless anal sex in the past year rose from about 44% in 2005 to 57% in 2011 and has kept rising; in the cities with the most PrEP it is now the large majority. Three things drove it in sequence: treatment made HIV survivable in the late 1990s, serosorting and viral-load knowledge replaced condoms among men who knew each other's status in the 2000s, and PrEP made the condom's HIV function redundant for the men taking it after 2012. Among heterosexuals the trend is flatter and driven by contraception: condoms compete with hormonal methods, and lost.

4. The projections

Four kinds of projection exist about condoms, and they say different things.

What condoms have done.

UNAIDS modelling credits condoms with about 117 million HIV infections averted since 1990, more than any other intervention including treatment. In generalised epidemics in southern Africa condoms are projected to remain the largest single contributor to infections averted through 2030, because they are cheap, need no clinic, and work against everything at once.

What the targets said.

The 2021 UNAIDS target was condom use in 95% of higher-risk sex by 2025. Surveys among sex workers and their clients report 60–80%; among young people in high-burden countries, 40–60%. Like every target in project 66, section 7, this one is missed, and condom procurement funding fell through the 2010s while the population at risk grew.

What falling condom use does to HIV among MSM.

The models agree that if PrEP coverage is high, condom decline barely moves HIV incidence: the men who stop using condoms are disproportionately the men on PrEP or with suppressed partners. Where PrEP coverage is low and uneven, as in the US South, the same decline raises incidence, which is the pattern in the CDC data: falling in white MSM in coastal cities, flat or rising in young Black and Hispanic MSM elsewhere.

What it does to everything else.

Here the models and the data agree in the other direction. PrEP protects against HIV only. Jenness et al. (2017) projected that PrEP with quarterly STI screening would reduce gonorrhoea and chlamydia among MSM despite less condom use, because of the testing, and that without the screening they would rise. US syphilis cases reached 209,000 in 2023, the most since 1950, before plateauing; rectal gonorrhoea among MSM roughly doubled between 2010 and 2019. Doxycycline after sex (doxyPEP, recommended 2024) is the response, and is itself a projection: modelled resistance in gonorrhoea within a decade.

The market is roughly 27–30 billion condoms a year, about US$10–11 billion, projected by the trade to reach US$15 billion by 2030 on population growth and the Asian market, while per-capita use in rich countries falls. The Gates programme of 2013, which funded eleven attempts to build a condom people would prefer, produced no product in a decade; the object of 1920 is the object of 2026, and the reason condoms are losing to PrEP is not the material.

6. The instruction, checked

Project 66's card ends: "use lubricant on the outside; never oil with latex". Both hold. Water- or silicone-based lubricant on the outside roughly halves breakage during anal sex; inside the condom, a drop at the tip helps and more than that raises slippage. Oil, including coconut oil, petroleum jelly, hand lotion and most massage oils, weakens latex measurably within sixty seconds and increases breakage several-fold; it is safe with polyurethane and nitrile. Polyisoprene behaves like latex. Project 71 has the rest.

7. What this means for the sentence in project 66

The 70% is correct, sourced, and the least useful number on the page. It describes what condoms did in two American cohorts of men who used them imperfectly before PrEP existed. For a man who uses them well, with lubricant, the per-act protection is above 90%. For a population, the number that matters is not the efficacy but the trend in section 3, which is downward and will stay downward, and the projections in section 4 say that this is survivable for HIV where PrEP is common and is already costing a syphilis epidemic everywhere.
