Strategic bombingrole 1 of 319651972Fixed targets deep in enemy territory — power, ports, rail, POL, factories — to destroy capacity and will. Rolling Thunder tried it on an agrarian country that imported its weapons; Linebacker II tried it for eleven days on the two cities and got a signature.

Phases

1964–1965
Reprisal

Pierce Arrow after Tonkin (5 Aug 1964); Flaming Dart after Pleiku (Feb 1965); Barrel Roll over Laos begins in December 1964, and never gets a name in public.

1965–1968
Rolling Thunder

Sustained, graduated, politically restricted; 864,000 tons on the North; 922 aircraft lost; the North’s air defence grew under it from nothing to the densest in the world. Halted 31 October 1968 with nothing to show but the talks.

1968–1972
The Trail and Cambodia

Commando Hunt on the Trail — sensors, gunships, and about 3 million tons on Laos in all; Menu on the Cambodian sanctuaries, 3,875 secret sorties, then Freedom Deal; the interdiction never cut the flow below what Hanoi needed.

1972–1973
Linebacker

Linebacker I (May–Oct 1972) with laser-guided bombs and the mined harbour, against a conventional offensive it could actually hit; Linebacker II (18–29 Dec), 741 B-52 sorties, 15 B-52s lost, about 1,600 dead in Hanoi and Haiphong, and the Accords signed four weeks later. Then Cambodia until the law ran out.

Outcome

A strategic failure by its own measures: the North did not surrender, did not stop supporting the South’s insurgency, and never lost the Trail. Two things worked — close air support for troops in contact, and eleven days over Hanoi to move a signature that had been agreed in October anyway. Everything in between fell on peasants, trucks and jungle, and on Laos.

Open questions

  • Whether Linebacker II changed Hanoi’s terms at all, or only Saigon’s willingness to sign; the January text is almost the October text.
  • Whether an unrestricted campaign in 1965 — the Chiefs’ ninety-four targets in sixteen days — would have worked or brought China in; nobody knows, and the restraint was the point.
  • Why interdiction was kept up for eight years after its own analysts (the Jason group, 1966–67) reported it could not cut the flow.
