Phases

1917–1917
Bread

23–25 February: strikes and crowds, bread queues in a city of frozen railways; the Cossacks would not charge; the police were stoned. Nobody yet called it a revolution.

1917–1917
The order to fire

26 February: Khabalov's troops fired on Znamenskaya Square, about forty dead; that evening one company of the Pavlovsky mutinied and was disarmed. The Tsar prorogued the Duma the same day.

1917–1917
The garrison goes over

27 February: the Volynsky at dawn, then the regiments, then the arsenal and the prisons; the Tauride Palace filled with soldiers; two bodies made themselves in its two wings. By night the capital had no government but them.

1917–1917
The train

28 February – 3 March: the railways closed by telegram, the Tsar's train turned back, the generals' answers at Pskov, the abdication, Michael's refusal. A dynasty of three hundred and four years ended in a railway carriage in four days.

Outcome

The monarchy fell in eight days to a crowd that no party had called and a garrison that would not fire on it. The Duma and the Soviet each took a wing of the same palace, and the eight months between this room and the next were the argument over which wing was the government. October answered it.

Open questions

  • Whether it was spontaneous: the workers' districts had committees and the strike had a date, but the mutiny had none; the honest answer is that the street was organised and the revolution was not.
  • Whether the war did it: a city of two and a half million with the railways frozen and the bread at half ration is a sufficient cause; the war is why the railways were frozen.
  • Whether Michael's refusal on 3 March, not Nicholas's abdication on the 2nd, is the ending: the standard says the ending is the fixed point, and this room picks the abdication because it was signed; Michael's was conditional, and the condition was never met.
