PrEP: where the 1% went

Project 69 · a report on one sentence in project 66 · index · repo randomcase/prep-where-the-1-went

The question. Project 66 says daily oral PrEP "cuts acquisition by about 99% when taken as prescribed". A reader asked who the 1% was and whether they took it the wrong way. The answer is that the 99% is not a count of people; it is a modelled figure for perfect adherence, and the people who acquired HIV on PrEP fall into three groups, only one of which is the 1%. This report gives the trials, the model, the breakthrough cases, the errors in how the number is used, the injections that replaced the pill, and the rectal versions that exist.

1. Where the 99% comes from

No trial randomised people to "take it every day" against placebo and observed a 99% difference. The chain is: iPrEx (2010) found 44% fewer infections in everyone given the pill, because half the participants were not taking it. Among those with drug detectable in their blood the reduction was 92%. iPrEx OLE (2014) then measured tenofovir-diphosphate in dried blood spots, which records average dosing over the previous month, and found no infections at all among people whose levels corresponded to four or more doses a week. A pharmacokinetic model fitted to those data (Anderson et al. 2012) gives the curve below; 99% is the model's value at seven doses a week. The CDC adopted "about 99%" for its guidance in 2019, rounding the model, and that is the sentence in project 66.

Two things follow. The 99% carries no confidence interval of its own; it is a point on a model, and the trial data at the top of the curve are consistent with anything from about 86% to 100%. And "as prescribed" is doing all the work: the population figure in every trial is the adherence figure.

Read down the result column and the pattern is not "the drug works 99%". It is "the drug works when it is in the blood, and how often it is in the blood depends on who is being asked to take it and how". The women's trials of 2012–2015 failed on adherence and the women's trials of 2022–2024 succeeded on injections that removed the adherence question. The MSM trials in between are the story of getting from 44% to a number worth quoting.

3. The 1%: who got HIV on PrEP

People who acquire HIV while prescribed PrEP fall into three groups, and only the third is what the reader meant.

Not taking it.

The large majority. In every cohort, infections cluster in people with no or low drug levels at the visit before diagnosis. In the US demonstration projects about 80–86% of participants had protective levels at any given visit, and the infections were in the rest. Discontinuation within a year runs 30–50% in insurance data.

Already infected when they started.

PrEP started during an undiagnosed acute infection, when the antibody test is still negative. The two drugs are not enough to treat HIV, so the virus acquires resistance (M184V/I, occasionally K65R) within weeks. This is the main source of "PrEP resistance" in the literature and it is a testing problem, not a prevention failure. Guidelines now require an RNA test at the start.

Taking it, and infected anyway.

About a dozen well-documented cases worldwide through 2023, each written up because it was rare. Toronto 2016 (Knox): drug levels consistent with daily dosing for two years; the virus was resistant to both PrEP drugs before he met it. New York 2017 (Markowitz): the same, multidrug resistant. Amsterdam 2017 (Hoornenborg): a wild-type, fully susceptible virus in a man with protective drug levels, after a very high number of receptive exposures with rectal STIs in the same period; the one case that cannot be explained by resistance. North Carolina 2018, Seattle 2019, and a few more in the same two patterns.

Arithmetic on the third group: about a dozen cases against roughly 3–4 million person-years of documented PrEP use worldwide by 2023 is on the order of one infection per 300,000 person-years, or below 0.001% a year. The 1% in "99%" is the model's residual, not a count; the count is two orders of magnitude smaller, and most of it is resistant virus, which no dose would have stopped.

The injection has its own version of the third group. In HPTN 083, four men acquired HIV with cabotegravir injections on time and adequate drug levels; the virus in most developed integrase-inhibitor resistance because the drug was present but, for reasons still argued (very high exposure, individual pharmacokinetics), not enough. The long-acting drugs also delay diagnosis: they suppress the virus without clearing it, so the antibody test turns positive weeks to months late, a state the trials named LEVI, long-acting early viral inhibition. Testing on injectable PrEP therefore needs RNA, not antibody.

4. The errors of calculus

Per year is not per act. The 99% is a relative reduction in the annual hazard of infection, from cohort follow-up. Project 66 multiplies it into a per-act number (138 × 0.01 = 1.4 per 10,000 acts). That is an approximation: the per-act figure assumes protection is uniform across acts, when in fact the drug concentration in rectal tissue varies with the days since the last dose and is much higher there than in vaginal tissue, which is why the 2-1-1 regimen works for anal sex and is not recommended for vaginal.
Intention-to-treat is not efficacy, and per-protocol is not either. The 44% counts everyone randomised and measures what the pill did in that population, including the people who did not take it; that is the honest public-health number and the one that predicts what a programme will achieve. The 92% counts only people with drug in their blood, which is a subgroup chosen after the fact by a behaviour, adherence, that also correlates with everything else protective. The true efficacy of a taken pill is between the two and closer to the second; it is not a measured quantity.
Zero at the top of the curve. "No infections at four or more doses a week" in iPrEx OLE is a small numerator over a few hundred person-years. As in project 68, zero events gives a ceiling, not a value. The 96% and 99% are model outputs through that zero.
Comparing to a pill is not comparing to nothing. HPTN 083's "66% better" and PURPOSE 2's "89% better" are against active daily PrEP, whose own efficacy in the comparison arm depends on how well those participants took it. Against a placebo, which no ethics committee will now allow, the absolute numbers would be higher for both arms and the relative gap could be anything. PURPOSE solved this with a "counterfactual placebo": a recency assay at screening estimates how many infections the population would have had with nothing, and the trial arm is compared to that estimate. It is a modelled comparator, and the 100% is 100% against a model.
Self-report is not drug level. VOICE participants reported 90% adherence; blood said under 30%. Every adherence figure not backed by a drug assay should be read as a statement about what people say at a clinic.

5. Did anyone take it the wrong way

The reader's phrase was whether the 1% "boofed it". Rectal administration of PrEP drugs is a real and active research line, not a mistake anyone in the trials made.

  • MTN-017 (2017): a reduced-glycerin 1% tenofovir gel applied rectally, daily or before sex, in 195 MSM and trans women on three continents. Safe, acceptable, and it reached protective tissue concentrations; efficacy was not tested. The earlier MTN-006 had shown the vaginal formulation of the same gel caused rectal side-effects because of its osmolality, which is where the reduced-glycerin version came from and why project 71 exists.
  • The tenofovir douche (DREAM, 2019–): Johns Hopkins formulated tenofovir as a rectal enema, on the reasoning in project 66, section 6: half of receptive partners already douche, so put the drug in the douche. Phase 1 showed a single dose produced tissue concentrations that blocked HIV in explants for at least 24 hours. Phase 2 is under way. None is licensed.
  • Oral PrEP taken rectally: there is no study, because there is no reason; the pill is formulated to be absorbed from the gut and would be, slowly, from the rectum, at an unknown dose. Nobody in the case reports did this.

The gap between 99% and the population is in this table, not in the pill. A drug that is 99% effective in the blood and in the blood of 36% of the people who need it, half of whom stop within a year, prevents a fraction of what the model says, which is why the projections in project 66 missed, and why the twice-yearly injection is the number the next projection will be built on.
