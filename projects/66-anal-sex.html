Scope. This report treats anal sex as a common practice with a measurable epidemiology: who reports it, how often, what the body is being asked to do, which harms are documented and at what rates, and which precautions have evidence behind them. It is written for adults and is descriptive, not prescriptive. Every number below has a source in section 10; where the sources disagree, the range is given. 2. What the body is being asked to do

The anatomy

The anal canal is about 3–4 cm long and is closed by two rings of muscle: an internal sphincter that is involuntary and holds tone all day, and an external one under conscious control. Beyond it the rectum runs 12–15 cm, turns twice, and is lined with a single layer of columnar cells, thinner and more vascular than the many-layered lining of the vagina.

No lubrication

The rectum produces a little mucus and nothing else. Friction without added lubricant tears the lining. This is the single fact from which most of section 3 follows.

The internal sphincter

It cannot be relaxed on command. It relaxes in response to pressure and time, and clamps in response to pain and fear. The practical consequence: slowness is not a courtesy, it is the mechanism.

Pleasure

The anus is densely innervated; in men the prostate sits 5–7 cm in on the front wall; in women the same wall borders the vagina and the internal parts of the clitoris. People who report enjoying it report these, and people who report not enjoying it usually report section 4.

3. The risks, with their numbers

HIV. Receptive anal intercourse is the highest per-act sexual risk measured. The CDC's pooled estimate is 138 transmissions per 10,000 exposures to an untreated HIV-positive partner; insertive anal is 11; receptive vaginal is 8; insertive vaginal is 4. The reasons are the thin lining, the tearing, and the density of CD4 target cells in the rectal mucosa. Three things change these numbers by an order of magnitude or more, and section 5 gives them.

Other infections. Rectal gonorrhoea and chlamydia are usually without symptoms, are missed entirely by a urine test, and are found in about 10–20% of men who have sex with men at screening. Syphilis, herpes, and hepatitis B pass the same way. Oral-anal contact carries hepatitis A, shigella, giardia, and other gut organisms; the outbreaks of shigella among gay and bisexual men in Britain and the United States since 2010 are the recent example. Mpox in 2022 spread mostly through sexual contact and presented rectally in many cases.

HPV and anal cancer. Most sexually active adults carry HPV at some point; the anus clears it less well than the cervix. Anal cancer is rare in the general population, about 2 per 100,000 a year, but runs at roughly 85 per 100,000 among HIV-positive men who have sex with men, a rate comparable to cervical cancer before screening. The ANCHOR trial (2022) showed that finding and treating the high-grade precursor lesions cut progression to cancer by more than half, which is why screening guidelines for that group exist now.

Mechanical injury. Small tears (fissures) and irritated haemorrhoids are common and heal. Perforation of the rectum is rare, is a surgical emergency, and in the literature is nearly always associated with objects rather than partners. Objects without a flared base account for a steady trickle of emergency visits; the rectum pulls inward.

Continence. One population analysis (NHANES 2005–2010) found faecal incontinence more common among adults who reported anal intercourse: about 2.8 times the odds in men and 1.5 in women, after adjustment. The study is cross-sectional and cannot say which way the arrow points, and most people who report anal sex report no such problem. It is reported here because it is the finding most often left out.

Pregnancy. None from the act itself; semen near the vaginal opening carries a small risk.

4. Pain, and who reports it

The gap between the first and third bars is the report's most important number. Men who have receptive anal sex are, on average, more practised, more likely to use lubricant, and more likely to be in a setting where stopping is easy. A qualitative study of 16–18-year-olds in England (Marston and Lewis, 2014) found young women expecting anal sex to hurt, young men expecting to persuade, and both treating pain as normal. Pain is not normal; it is the internal sphincter doing its job, and it is the signal that the mechanism in section 2 is being overridden.

5. What lowers the risks, and by how much

Treatment as preventionlargest effect

An HIV-positive partner with a suppressed viral load on treatment transmitted zero infections in about 77,000 condomless acts in the PARTNER and PARTNER2 studies. This is the largest single reduction known.

PrEP~99%

Daily oral PrEP cuts acquisition by about 99% when taken as prescribed; the long-acting injection is at least as effective. Adherence is the whole story.

Condoms~70%

Consistent use reduces HIV transmission through anal sex by about 70%, less than the often-quoted vaginal figure, because breakage and slippage are more common. Use lubricant on the outside; never oil with latex.

Lubricant<1,200 mOsm/kg

Water- or silicone-based, and plenty of it. Some water-based products are strongly hyperosmolar and strip the rectal lining in the laboratory; the WHO advisory (2012) recommends products under about 1,200 mOsm/kg. Nonoxynol-9 damages rectal tissue and should not be used there.

VaccinesHPV · HAV · HBV · mpox

HPV (routine through 26, shared decision to 45), hepatitis A, hepatitis B, and mpox where indicated. The HPV vaccine given before exposure is the only intervention that prevents anal cancer at the source.

Testingswab, not urine

Rectal swabs for gonorrhoea and chlamydia for anyone reporting receptive anal sex, every three to twelve months depending on partners; a urine test alone misses most rectal infections. Doxycycline after sex (doxyPEP) is now recommended for some groups to prevent syphilis and chlamydia.

Douchingcontested

About half of men who have sex with men rinse before receptive sex. Plain water in a small volume is what the evidence tolerates; soaps and repeated large-volume rinses irritate the lining, and several studies associate douching with more rectal infections, though whether it causes them is not settled.

Pace, position, stoppinguntrialled

Not measured in trials, but every clinical guide says the same: the receptive partner controls depth and speed, external stimulation first, stop at pain. Section 4 is the evidence that this advice is not being followed.

6. Douching, and what it does to the HIV numbers

How common. Between about half and two-thirds of men who have sex with men report rinsing the rectum before receptive sex, most with tap water from a bulb or shower attachment, some with commercial enema products. Surveys of women who have anal sex find lower but not trivial rates.

What the studies find. A meta-analysis of 24 studies (Li et al. 2019) put the odds of HIV infection among men who douche at about 2.8 times those who do not, and the odds of a rectal gonorrhoea or chlamydia infection at about 2.4 times. The finding is consistent across studies and has three candidate explanations, which are not exclusive: douching strips the mucus layer and can tear the single-cell lining, especially with soap, hyperosmolar products, or hot water; it disturbs the rectal microbiome, which in laboratory work affects how easily HIV crosses the mucosa; and men who douche have, on average, more receptive partners, so some of the association is who douches rather than the douching. The studies are mostly cross-sectional and adjust for partner numbers imperfectly, so the 2.8 is an upper bound on the causal effect, not the effect.

What follows. Guidance that treats douching as fixed behaviour rather than something to forbid has settled on: plain water or an isotonic saline, at body temperature, a small volume, once, well before sex; no soap, no commercial hyperosmolar enemas, no repeated rinsing. Two trials of a rectal douche that carries a PrEP drug (a tenofovir enema, DREAM and follow-on studies) have shown the product is acceptable and reaches the tissue; the idea is to turn the habit into the protection. None is licensed yet.

7. Is transmission tracking the projections?

Short answer: no, in both the United States and the world, and by a wide margin. The tools in section 5 work as well as the trials said they would; the projections assumed they would reach far more people than they have.

Why the gap. Four reasons recur in the reviews. The tools reach the people already connected to care and miss the people who are not: in the United States, about 40% of new infections come from people who do not know they have HIV, and another 40% from people diagnosed but not in care. PrEP uptake tracks insurance and geography, not risk. Sexually transmitted infections rose through the 2010s, which is both a marker of condomless sex and, for syphilis and rectal gonorrhoea, a multiplier of HIV transmission. And the projections were made before a pandemic that closed clinics for a year and before the 2025 funding cuts.

What moved the other way. Twice-yearly injectable lenacapavir was approved in 2025 after two trials (PURPOSE 1 and 2) in which it prevented essentially every infection; the question the projections now hinge on is whether it reaches the people the daily pill did not. Where PrEP and treatment did scale, in London, San Francisco, Sydney, New South Wales, and among gay men in several European cities, new diagnoses fell by half or more, which is the evidence that the projections were achievable in principle.

The per-act figures in section 3 have not changed since 2014; what changed is how many acts happen under section 5. That is the whole answer to the question in this section's title.

8. Rape, coercion, and HIV

The honest first sentence is that no surveillance system counts it. HIV case reports are filed by "transmission category" (male-to-male sexual contact, injection, heterosexual contact), and none of the categories asks whether the contact was consented to. What exists instead is three kinds of evidence, each answering a different question.

The claim that every gay man who acquires HIV was raped is false, and no dataset comes near it. The table gives what is measured: gay and bisexual men report sexual violence at roughly twice the rate of heterosexual men and at about the rate of heterosexual women, and a minority, not a majority, of gay men report ever having been raped. Among men who have sex with men who acquire HIV, the studies that ask about the likely exposure event find it overwhelmingly to be consensual receptive anal sex with a partner whose status was unknown or misreported; assault appears in the low single digits of percent where it is recorded at all. The per-act arithmetic in section 3 explains why the consensual route dominates: 138 per 10,000 acts, repeated over years of partners, produces most infections without any coercion, and the surveillance systems that could count coerced exposures do not ask.

Women's infections are the mirror image in one respect: consensual sex with a husband or regular partner is the main route worldwide, and the violence that matters statistically is the intimate-partner kind in the third row of the table above, which raises the rate by about half rather than accounting for it.

What follows from it. The medical response is post-exposure prophylaxis: a 28-day course of antiretrovirals started within 72 hours, offered as standard after sexual assault in most high-income countries and recommended by WHO everywhere. Its effectiveness after sexual exposure is estimated, not trialled, at about 80% when started promptly and completed; completion rates after assault are poor, around 40%, which is the gap the services work on. The epidemiological response is the finding in the third row: where violence against women is common, HIV prevention that does not address it is working against a current.

The proportion, plainly. Rape is not a major route of HIV transmission by count in any country with data. It is a major route of harm to the person it happens to, and in the places where it is organised, it is a route the count has not been able to see.

9. Law and the longer record

  1. 1533 — England's Buggery Act (25 Hen. 8 c. 6) makes the act a capital offence, the first secular statute in Europe to do so.
    • R v Lord Hungerford, 1540 — Walter Hungerford, Baron Hungerford of Heytesbury, the first man executed under the Act, beheaded with Thomas Cromwell on the same day; buggery was one count among treason.
    • R v Udall, 1541 — Nicholas Udall, headmaster of Eton, confessed to buggery with a pupil; sentence reduced to a year's imprisonment, and he later became headmaster of Westminster.
    • R v Castlehaven, 1631 — Mervyn Touchet, 2nd Earl of Castlehaven, tried by his peers for rape of his wife and sodomy with a servant; beheaded. The case set the rule that the Act reached a peer and did not require proof of emission (later reversed, then restored by statute in 1828).
    • R v Wiseman, 1716 — held that the Act covered the act with a woman as well as a man.
    • R v Pratt and Smith, 1835 — James Pratt and John Smith, hanged at Newgate on 27 November 1835, the last two men executed for the offence in England; observed through a keyhole by a landlord. Dickens saw them in the condemned cell and wrote it up in Sketches by Boz. Both pardoned posthumously in 2017.
    • Offences Against the Person Act 1861 — the death penalty replaced by ten years to life; the first Act to reduce it.
  2. 1860–1900 — the Indian Penal Code's section 377, drafted by Macaulay in 1837 and enacted in 1860, and its copies carry the offence into every British colony; most of today's laws descend from this one clause.
    • Queen-Empress v Khairati, Allahabad High Court, 1884 — the first reported prosecution under 377, of a hijra; conviction quashed for want of evidence of a specific act, but the judgment fixed the reading that "against the order of nature" meant the act itself.
    • Naz Foundation v Government of NCT of Delhi, Delhi High Court, 2009 — read 377 down to exclude consenting adults in private.
    • Suresh Kumar Koushal v Naz Foundation, Supreme Court of India, 2013 — reversed Naz and restored the section, calling the affected population "a minuscule fraction".
    • Navtej Singh Johar v Union of India, Supreme Court of India, 6 September 2018 — five judges unanimously struck 377 as applied to consenting adults, overruling Koushal; the judgment is now cited in the courts of the other former colonies below.
    • Copies: Straits Settlements Penal Code 1871 (later Singapore 377A, 1938), Queensland Criminal Code 1899 (exported to Nigeria, Kenya, Uganda, Tanzania, Papua New Guinea, and the Pacific), the Criminal Code Ordinance 1936 (Palestine, Cyprus), the Sudan Penal Code 1899. The full descent is in the country table below.
  3. 1961 — Illinois becomes the first US state to repeal its sodomy law, on the Model Penal Code's advice.
    • A statute, not a case: the Illinois Criminal Code of 1961, effective 1 January 1962, adopted the American Law Institute's Model Penal Code tentative draft No. 4 (1955), which had recommended removing consensual sodomy from the criminal law.
    • Before it: Rose v Locke (1975) and Wainwright v Stone (1973) later upheld vague "crime against nature" statutes elsewhere; Connecticut was the second state to repeal, in 1969, and twenty states followed in the 1970s.
    • The Wolfenden Report (1957) in Britain, recommending the same, was the document the ALI drafters cited.
  4. 1967 — England and Wales decriminalise, in private, between two men over 21. The exceptions took thirty-six more years to remove.
    • R v Turing, Knutsford Quarter Sessions, 1952 — Alan Turing convicted of gross indecency under section 11 of the Criminal Law Amendment Act 1885, the companion offence to buggery; probation on condition of hormone treatment. Royal pardon 2013; the "Turing law" in the Policing and Crime Act 2017 pardoned about 49,000 others.
    • R v Montagu, Wildeblood and Pitt-Rivers, Winchester Assizes, 1954 — Lord Montagu of Beaulieu, Peter Wildeblood, and Michael Pitt-Rivers imprisoned for offences with two airmen; the public reaction to the trial produced the Wolfenden Committee, whose 1957 report produced the 1967 Act.
    • Sexual Offences Act 1967 — legal only in private, only between two men, only over 21, not in Scotland, Northern Ireland, the armed forces, or the merchant navy. Prosecutions for offences outside these limits rose after 1967.
    • Dudgeon v United Kingdom, European Court of Human Rights, 1981 — the Northern Ireland law breached Article 8; decriminalised there in 1982. Scotland had followed in 1981 by the Criminal Justice (Scotland) Act 1980.
    • Age of consent, 21 to 18: Criminal Justice and Public Order Act 1994, after the Commons rejected 16 by 307 to 280.
    • Sutherland v United Kingdom, European Commission of Human Rights, 1997 — Euan Sutherland's complaint that 18 against 16 for heterosexual sex was discriminatory was upheld; the government undertook to equalise. The Lords blocked the bill twice; the Parliament Acts were used, and the Sexual Offences (Amendment) Act 2000 set 16 from 8 January 2001.
    • Smith and Grady v United Kingdom and Lustig-Prean and Beckett v United Kingdom, ECHR, 1999 — the armed-forces ban breached Article 8; lifted 12 January 2000.
    • ADT v United Kingdom, ECHR, 2000 — the "in private" rule, read to exclude more than two men, breached Article 8; the Sexual Offences Act 2003 then abolished buggery and gross indecency as offences altogether, in force 1 May 2004.
  5. 2003 — Lawrence v Texas strikes down the remaining thirteen US state laws.
    • Bowers v Hardwick, 478 U.S. 186 (1986) — Michael Hardwick, arrested in his Atlanta bedroom by an officer serving a warrant for an unpaid ticket; the Court upheld Georgia's law 5–4, Justice White finding no fundamental right "to engage in homosexual sodomy". Justice Powell later said he had probably made a mistake.
    • Powell v State, Supreme Court of Georgia, 1998 — the same Georgia statute struck under the state constitution's privacy right, twelve years after Bowers had upheld it federally.
    • Commonwealth v Wasson, Kentucky, 1992; Gryczan v State, Montana, 1997; Campbell v Sundquist, Tennessee, 1996 — state courts striking their own laws while Bowers stood.
    • Lawrence v Texas, 539 U.S. 558 (2003) — John Geddes Lawrence and Tyron Garner, arrested in Houston on 17 September 1998 after a false weapons report by a neighbour, fined $200 each under the Texas Homosexual Conduct law. The Court, 6–3, Justice Kennedy writing, overruled Bowers: "Bowers was not correct when it was decided, and it is not correct today." Thirteen state laws fell: Alabama, Florida, Idaho, Kansas, Louisiana, Michigan, Mississippi, Missouri, North Carolina, Oklahoma, South Carolina, Texas, Utah, and Virginia (fourteen by some counts, since Virginia's covered all sodomy).
    • After: several states left the dead statutes on the books; Texas still had its in 2025. Justice Thomas's concurrence in Dobbs v Jackson (2022) named Lawrence as a precedent the Court should "reconsider"; no petition has yet been taken up.
  6. 2018–2025 — India, Botswana, Singapore, Antigua, St Kitts, Barbados, Mauritius, the Cook Islands, Dominica, and Namibia repeal or strike; Trinidad's court puts its law back; Mali, Burkina Faso, and Iraq add new ones. About sixty-four jurisdictions criminalise it between men, twelve retain the death penalty on the books, and in six it has been carried out or is a live sentence. The act is older than the word for it. It appears in Egyptian, Greek, Roman, Indian, Japanese, and Mesoamerican sources, mostly in art and satire, occasionally in law. The Christian and later Islamic legal traditions grouped it under sodomy and criminalised it regardless of the partner's sex; the list above is what happened to that inheritance in the one empire whose codes travelled furthest.
