Phases

1961–1961
Bay of Pigs

Brigade 2506 lands 17 April 1961; Castro’s militia and the air force he still had beat it in three days; 1,189 captured, ransomed in December 1962 for $53 million in food and medicine.

1962–1962
Anadyr

Khrushchev decides in May; ships sail from July; missiles arrive in September; the U-2 photographs them on 14 October.

1962–1962
Thirteen days

16–28 October: the ExComm, the quarantine, the letters, the U-2 shot down, the Jupiter trade.

1962–1962
The Cuban response

Castro refuses UN inspection; his five points; the IL-28 bombers argued over into November; Mikoyan in Havana for three weeks; the quarantine lifted 20 November.

The spread, one line per day

1962-10-01WashingtonMcNamara orders CINCLANT to be ready for a Cuba blockade or strike by 20 October; the readiness date is a coincidence the tapes never mention.
1962-10-02HavanaCastro tells a rally that Cuba will be defended; the missiles are not mentioned, on Moscow’s instruction, and he resents the instruction.
1962-10-03WashingtonCongress passes the joint resolution on Cuba: force is authorised if Cuba threatens the hemisphere.
1962-10-04MoscowThe Indigirka docks at Mariel with the nuclear warheads aboard; no one in Washington knows.
1962-10-05WashingtonMcCone, back from his honeymoon, argues the Soviets would not build SAM sites unless there was something to hide; the U-2s are grounded over western Cuba because of SAM risk.
1962-10-06WashingtonContingency orders to Atlantic Command: prepare for air strikes and an invasion.
1962-10-07HavanaCuban President Dorticós at the UN: if attacked, Cuba has the weapons it wishes it did not need. It is taken as bluster.
1962-10-08WashingtonDorticós’s speech is read at State and set aside.
1962-10-09WashingtonKennedy approves a U-2 flight over western Cuba, weather permitting.
1962-10-10WashingtonSenator Keating says on the floor that there are missile bases in Cuba; the administration says there is no evidence.
1962-10-11HavanaSoviet units finish the San Cristóbal sites’ concrete; Cuban troops are kept out of the compounds.
1962-10-12WashingtonResponsibility for U-2 flights over Cuba is transferred from the CIA to SAC.
1962-10-13WashingtonBad weather; the flight is postponed.
1962-10-14WashingtonMajor Heyser’s U-2 flies over San Cristóbal at 0731 and takes 928 photographs in six minutes.
1962-10-15WashingtonNPIC analysts identify SS-4 medium-range launchers by evening; Bundy decides to let the president sleep.
1962-10-16WashingtonBundy tells Kennedy at 0845. First ExComm meeting at 1150, taped. Kennedy: “I don’t know quite what kind of a world we live in after we’ve struck Cuba.” McNamara sets out three tracks: talk, blockade, strike. Rusk, first draft: “We could carry out an air strike without any warning.” No decision.
1962-10-17WashingtonKennedy keeps his schedule in Connecticut to hide the crisis; the ExComm meets without him; McNamara moves to the blockade.
1962-10-18WashingtonGromyko in the Oval Office says Soviet aid to Cuba is defensive; Kennedy, with the photographs in his desk, does not show them. Evening tape, Kennedy dictating: the consensus is for a blockade, “since it puts the burden on the Soviets.”
1962-10-19WashingtonKennedy meets the Joint Chiefs, taped. LeMay: the blockade is “almost as bad as the appeasement at Munich”; Kennedy replies that if the Soviets take Berlin “I’m sure you’d all recommend the use of nuclear weapons,” and leaves. Afterward, off tape, to O’Donnell: the Chiefs have one advantage, that if he listens to them none of them will be alive to say they were wrong. That is the Chiefs’ position for the whole crisis; it is not restated below.
1962-10-20WashingtonKennedy flies back from Chicago with a “cold”. Formal decision: quarantine, not strike. Adlai Stevenson argues for offering the Jupiters and Guantánamo; he is shouted down and half of it happens anyway.
1962-10-21HavanaSoviet General Pliyev reports the R-12 regiments nearly ready; Castro is not shown the readiness reports.
1962-10-22WashingtonKennedy to the nation at 1900: the missiles, the quarantine, and “a full retaliatory response upon the Soviet Union” for any launch from Cuba. DEFCON 3. Congressional leaders, briefed at 1700, wanted invasion; Fulbright and Russell both.
1962-10-22HavanaCastro orders general mobilisation; 270,000 under arms within days. Cuban radio calls it aggression.
1962-10-23MoscowKhrushchev’s letter: the quarantine is piracy; ships will not comply. Privately he orders the ships carrying missiles to stop.
1962-10-23WashingtonOAS votes 19–0 for the quarantine; Kennedy signs the proclamation at 1906. Robert Kennedy sees Dobrynin at the embassy that night.
1962-10-24WashingtonQuarantine in force at 1000. SAC goes to DEFCON 2, in the clear, so Moscow can hear. The ExComm learns that the Soviet ships nearest the line have stopped or turned. Rusk to Bundy: “We’re eyeball to eyeball, and I think the other fellow just blinked.”
1962-10-25WashingtonStevenson at the UN to Zorin: “I am prepared to wait for my answer until hell freezes over.” The tanker Bucharest is let through. Kennedy, on tape, on how the Soviet leader might see it: he does not want a war either.
1962-10-26MoscowKhrushchev’s long letter, dictated, arrives in four parts through the evening: the missiles will go if the US promises not to invade. “We and you ought not now to pull on the ends of the rope in which you have tied the knot of war.”
1962-10-26HavanaCastro, after a night at the Soviet embassy, writes to Khrushchev: if the imperialists invade, “that would be the moment to eliminate such danger forever” — a first strike. The letter reaches Moscow the next day and frightens Khrushchev more than Kennedy did.
1962-10-27Washington“Black Saturday.” A second Khrushchev letter, public, adds the Jupiters in Turkey. Major Anderson’s U-2 is shot down over Banes by a Soviet SAM crew acting without Moscow’s order; another U-2 strays over Chukotka. The ExComm, taped, on the shootdown; McNamara: “this is a change in the pattern.” Kennedy decides to answer the first letter and ignore the second. Robert Kennedy to Dobrynin at 1945: withdraw and there will be no invasion; the Jupiters will go in four or five months, and that part must never be said aloud.
1962-10-27HavanaCastro orders Cuban anti-aircraft to fire on low-level flights; Cuban guns hit an RF-8. He is told the U-2 was shot down by Soviets and is pleased.
1962-10-28MoscowKhrushchev’s answer broadcast on Radio Moscow at 0900 Washington time, before the letter can be delivered, to be sure it arrives in time: the missiles will be dismantled and crated.
1962-10-28HavanaCastro hears it on the radio. Guevara’s aide records that he kicked a wall and broke a mirror. Castro issues the five points: end the embargo, end the subversion, end the raids, end the overflights, return Guantánamo. None is granted.
1962-10-29WashingtonThe ExComm turns to verification; the Chiefs, on paper, call the Soviet message a cover for delay and recommend the strike proceed on the 30th unless there is “irrefutable evidence”. Kennedy files it.
1962-10-30HavanaU Thant arrives from the UN; Castro refuses inspection on Cuban soil. It is the Cuban response in one sentence: the deal was made over his head and will not be verified on his ground.
1962-10-31HavanaU Thant leaves with nothing. Kennedy suspends the quarantine for his visit and resumes it.
1962-11-01HavanaCastro on television for ninety minutes: the missiles were Cuba’s business; the inspection will not happen; the Soviets are comrades, and were wrong.
1962-11-02HavanaMikoyan arrives from Moscow to calm Castro; his wife dies in Moscow the next day and he stays in Havana.
1962-11-03HavanaMikoyan and Castro, first session: Castro repeats the five points; Mikoyan repeats that the missiles are Soviet property.
1962-11-04WashingtonLow-level reconnaissance continues; the ExComm now argues about the IL-28 bombers, which Kennedy had called offensive on the 22nd and Khrushchev had not mentioned on the 28th.
1962-11-05HavanaSoviet ships begin loading the R-12s at Mariel and Casilda, on deck, uncrated, so the Navy can photograph them at sea.
1962-11-06WashingtonMidterm elections; the Democrats hold the Senate and lose four House seats, less than expected.
1962-11-07WashingtonDestroyers pull alongside Soviet freighters at sea; tarpaulins are drawn back; the missiles are counted. Forty-two, the number Moscow gave.
1962-11-08HavanaCastro tells Mikoyan the Luna tactical rockets were promised to Cuba and should stay; Mikoyan says no, and later that no nuclear weapon will stay.
1962-11-09WashingtonThe last R-12s leave Cuban waters. The gauge reads zero. The Chiefs ask that the quarantine continue until the IL-28s go.
1962-11-10HavanaMikoyan, again: the bombers. Castro: not for the Americans’ asking.
1962-11-11WashingtonKennedy, on tape, on the bombers: they matter less than the missiles and more than nothing; he will not invade over them and will not lift the quarantine without them.
1962-11-12MoscowKhrushchev offers to remove the IL-28s “in due course”; Kennedy wants a date.
1962-11-13HavanaCastro tells Mikoyan he will keep the anti-aircraft guns firing at low-level flights; Mikoyan asks him not to; the flights continue and the guns do not fire.
1962-11-14WashingtonRobert Kennedy to Dobrynin: thirty days for the bombers, or the quarantine stays.
1962-11-15HavanaCastro writes to U Thant that Cuban forces will fire on overflights from now on. They do not.
1962-11-16HavanaMikoyan, last sessions: Castro agrees not to obstruct the bombers’ removal; he does not agree to it.
1962-11-17MoscowKhrushchev decides the IL-28s will go within thirty days.
1962-11-18WashingtonThe ExComm drafts the announcement; the Chiefs ask that the no-invasion pledge be conditional on inspection. It is.
1962-11-19MoscowKhrushchev’s note: the bombers within thirty days; Kennedy takes it.
1962-11-20WashingtonKennedy’s press conference: the bombers are going; the quarantine is lifted tonight; SAC stands down from DEFCON 2 the next day. Cuba was not at the table on any of the fifty-one days.

Outcome

Missiles out, bombers out, Jupiters out in April 1963 without announcement, no invasion, no inspection, Castro in power for another forty-six years, and a hotline.

Open questions

  • Whether Kennedy would have traded the Jupiters publicly on the 29th if Khrushchev had not accepted on the 28th; Rusk’s 1987 letter says the Cordier channel was ready.
  • What Pliyev would have done with the Lunas if the invasion had come; the 1962 order allowing local use was withdrawn on 22 October, and the withdrawal reached him.
  • Whether the Cuban response — no inspection, the five points, the guns — changed anything, or only proved that the two large powers had settled it between themselves.
