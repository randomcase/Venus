The warlock archive is 180 directories holding 22 people and 158 empty shells. Two archives, two shapes, one absence.

Half of this registry is a number where a name should be. That is not the archive being careless — it is the archive being accurate about what was written down at the time. A trial of one burgomaster produced a docket, a letter and a name. A morning that killed seventy-one people in one parish produced a total.

The Spanish Inquisition stopped first, and stopped hardest. Granada ruled in 1526 that witchcraft was an illusion and banned the death penalty for spells. Alonso de Salazar Frías investigated 1,800 people in the Basque country and concluded it did not exist; the Edict of Silence halted executions across Spain and Latin America in 1614. Rome restricted the death penalty to proven poisoning in 1623.

Meanwhile the last person legally executed for witchcraft in Europe was beheaded in Protestant Glarus in 1782 — 256 years after the first of those rulings. Whatever the reflex says about which institution was the worst of them, the dates in this table do not support it.
The registry contains 2 instruments that authorised the machinery and 7 that restrained it. Both sets are declared by row number at the top of the generator, so this count can be audited against the markdown a line at a time.

Midwife is the commonest stated occupation in the registry by a wide margin — 11 rows, against 6 for the next. Counting herbalism and healing with it, 12 of the 31 rows that state an occupation state the same kind of work: attending births, growing plants, treating the sick. Against the warlock roster, where the men who were actually tried were a burgomaster, a judge, a minister, a schoolmaster, a priest and an officer, the shape is office on one side and skill on the other — and it matters for the exchange, because forfeiture only pays when the accused owns something. On the ones who owned nothing the same machinery ran anyway and produced a debt instead: Tituba was never convicted of anything and was sold to cover her own jail fees.
And the contrast does not hold cleanly, so the board says so. 4 rows break it. Lindheim, 1652: a local official executed twenty women in order to seize their estates. Katharina Henot was a wealthy postmistress, tortured and burned at Cologne in 1627 and formally exonerated by the city council in 2012. The estate motive sits on both sides of the line, and a board that dropped those rows to keep a tidy finding would be doing the thing these archives already do.
The bars count trial rows only. An instrument row can carry a person’s name and that person is its author — row 50 is Friedrich Spee, the Jesuit whose Cautio Criminalis published against judicial torture and helped end the German hysteria. Counting him among the accused would put a man who stopped this in the column of people it happened to. 1 named row is excluded on that ground.
One further row is not what it looks like. Row 61 names Pierre de Lancre, the royal judge who condemned them — the only row in the registry whose named person is the one doing the condemning. It is counted as an unnamed row here, because the people it killed are still not in the registry.

what this file refuses

Retyping the source. This board parses THE_100_HISTORIC_WITCH_TRIALS.md at build time. If the archive changes the board changes, and if the parse finds fewer than 100 rows the generator stops rather than publishing a short table and calling it the registry.
An undeclared classification — which rows name a person, which are instruments, and which way each instrument points are all written out as explicit row-number sets, so every count above can be checked against the markdown. And a tidy contrast: the 4 rows that break the office-versus-skill finding are counted and named rather than quietly dropped.
