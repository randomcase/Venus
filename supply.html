Δv is shown in tenths because CSS counters are integers and 3.5 is not one. It is not a trick to make a number look bigger — the unit is on the tile, and a page that rounded 3.5 to 4 to keep the label tidy would be lying about half a kilometre a second.

Path one the long arc

You wait, and then you send everything. A Hohmann transfer is the cheapest crossing that physics allows: the ship leaves when Earth and Venus are aligned for it, coasts a hundred and forty-six days on an ellipse that touches both orbits, and arrives having spent almost nothing beyond the departure burn. Nearly all of what you launched is still there to land.

The cost is the calendar. The alignment comes round every 584 days — the synodic period — so a missed window is nineteen months, not next month. Everything New Paris cannot make for itself has to be on that ship, and the station has to hold enough to live on until the next one. Path one is not slow shipping. It is a nineteen-month inventory problem wearing a ship.

Path two through the relay

You pay for the crossing out of the cargo. Leaving faster than the minimum-energy arc means burning harder, and every extra metre per second comes off what the same launcher can throw. Twelve tonnes off Earth lands as seven. Thirty lands as seventeen. The ship is not smaller — the fuel took the difference.

What you buy is not just speed, it is cadence. The relay is already in transfer space and is not waiting on your alignment, so departures come round about every 210 days instead of 584. Three sendings in the time path one manages one, each carrying less. For anything perishable, medical, or simply late, that is the only path that exists.

And peer to peer, at this distance, means store and forward. One-way light time between the two planets runs from 2.1 minutes at closest approach to 14.3 at furthest. Nothing out there is under live control by anyone, so the relay does not negotiate — it holds what it was given and passes it on when the geometry allows. That is not a protocol decision. It is the speed of light, and there is no version of this design in which it is different.

Which is why there are two. Neither path is better; they fail in opposite directions. Run only the long arc and the outpost is one missed launch from rationing. Run only the relay and it never receives a girder. New Paris is supplied by both because a single path is a single point of failure with a schedule attached — and the divergence is the whole design, not a hedge.

On the numbers

The 584-day synodic period and the ~146-day Hohmann transit are standard and well-established. The 3.5 km/s departure Δv is the usual planning figure from low Earth orbit; the 5.2 for a faster crossing is representative rather than the product of a specific trajectory, and the landed-mass reductions follow from the rocket equation at that Δv rather than from a real vehicle. Light times are exact for Venus at closest and furthest approach. Read the first three as facts, the rest as arithmetic done honestly on assumptions I have named.

One thing Venus gives you free. Arrival is into an atmosphere, at a station floating in it — so the last stage is aerocapture and a parachute rather than a landing burn. The planet that makes the surface impossible is the same planet that makes the delivery cheap.
